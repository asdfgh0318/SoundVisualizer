Reading the Room

How anechoic-chamber problems show up in measured data — standing waves, interference patterns, recirculation, shear layers — how the literature reads them, and what people actually do about them.

source texts govern where this brief disagrees

A chamber is never anechoic; it is anechoic in an envelope of frequency, distance and direction. Every paper collected here reaches the same conclusion by a different route: the number that matters is not the wedge absorption coefficient but the deviation from the inverse-square law measured with the rig in place, and that deviation is an interference pattern — direct wave plus a weak reflection — whose spatial and spectral shape tells you where the reflection comes from. This brief is organised around that pattern: what causes it (§1), how to read it (§2), how qualification methods can hide it (§3), and what has been done to shrink it (§4). §5 applies it to the arc rig; §6 is the evidence ledger.

01Where the deviation comes from

Interference, not absorption, is the observable

Cunefare et al. (JASA 2003) put it plainly: the deviation "is due to interference between the direct and reflected components of the wave field", so it varies "on length scales comparable to a fraction of a wavelength". Measured over 80 Hz–10 kHz, the mean spacing of zero-crossings of the deviation was 29–41 % of a wavelength (40.8 % at 80 Hz, 29.2 % at 1–2 kHz). ● Jiang, Zhang & Huang (JSV 2016) explain why a "99 % absorbing" wedge still produces this: "99 % energy absorption means 10 % pressure reflection, which is still very large. In some area inside the anechoic chamber, the multi-reflected sound accumulates and violates the 1.5 dB deviation easily." In their 9 × 8 × 7 m simulation, wedges designed for 100 Hz keep only 57 % of the centre-to-corner line inside ±1.5 dB at 100 Hz; the effective cut-off by a 75 %-of-line criterion is 128 Hz — 28 % above the design value. ●

Below the cut-off: modes, not free field

Nardari et al. (AIAA 2019) calibrated Amazon's hemi-anechoic rotor room with a loudspeaker: within ±1 dB above 2.2 × BPF "but only within ±4 dB below that, primarily due to the onset of standing waves, which are a consequence of the nearly cuboidal shape of the chamber". ● Schneider (JSV 2009) measured the LMA Marseille chamber (5.4 × 6.3 × 11.4 m, 0.7 m melamine wedges) with pure tones on a 0.2 m grid: "significant deviations from free-field conditions occur in the 100–160 Hz frequency range" and the room "was found to be asymmetric in the 170–190 Hz frequency range". ● Haasjes (PhD, Twente 2025) reads that case as a chamber "designed for 75 Hz" whose real cut-off "lies at 200 Hz rather than 75 Hz", with the lined doors among the identified causes, and generalises from the literature: chambers "suffer from low-frequency sound waves (up to about 200 Hz) reflecting off the walls, in some cases even above the cut off frequency the chamber is designed for". ● Two small chambers show what the collapse looks like in numbers. Singh, Garg & Narayanan (Int. J. Aeroacoustics 2020, 2.6 × 1.7 × 2.2 m, 0.3 m PU wedges): the fitted decay per doubling of distance is 0.29 / 0.12 dB at 100 Hz and 3.02 / 3.65 dB at 200 Hz in the two corner paths, against 5.67 / 6.17 dB at 315 Hz, which they declare the cut-off "within 0.5 dB". ● Garg et al. (MAPAN 2019, 2 m³ transportable chamber, 0.35 m wedges): at 125 Hz the deviation runs from +4.52 dB at 0.64 m through 0.06 dB at 0.84 m to −7.13 dB at 1.14 m, so the ISO 26101 tolerance holds "between 79 and 89 cm" only — a 10 cm working space. ●

Tones expose what band noise hides

This is the single most repeated finding. Cunefare: at 500 Hz, 37 % of a tonal corner traverse was out of tolerance against 0 % for band-limited noise on the same span; broadband qualification is "a marginal indicator" and "of dubious value" for chambers used with tonal sources. ● NPL (Simmons, Jobling & Payne, DQL-AC 007) stripped a hemi-anechoic room wedge by wedge: pure-tone deviations reached 9.3 dB at 250 Hz and 14.3 dB at 8 kHz while broadband never exceeded 3.7 dB, "most likely due to averaging of the interference between the limits of the frequency band being analysed". ● Nash (ICA 2019) passed a 51 m³ commercial chamber on pink noise and failed it on tones: the 2056 Hz draw-away fitted −26.4 dB/decade, "a value that is physically impossible", read as "an acoustical cancellation occurring between the arriving wave front and a strong reflection from a nearby surface". ●

Palchikovskiy et al. (AIP Conf. Proc. 2016) report the same asymmetry as a facility spec for the Perm aeroacoustic chamber: "a free acoustic field within radius of 2 m for tonal noise and 3 m for broadband noise", with microphones "at least at the distance of 1 m from the tips of sound-absorbing wedges". ● Drone noise is tonal, dominated by blade-passage harmonics. A chamber qualified on noise has not been qualified for this work.

Discontinuities and fixtures

Failures found in practice are rarely "bad wedges everywhere". Rizzi et al. (NASA SALT, 337 m³): all ISO 3745 points passed "except the farthest measurement locations below 250 Hz … close to the room corner opposite the sound source", attributed to "long wavelength sound reflecting from the corners". ● Winker & Stahnke (Inter-Noise 2016): above 10 kHz "the most likely factor is the influence of the measurement system inside the free-field creating reflections that do not appear at lower frequencies … This situation occurs during pure tone qualifications and is not present in broadband qualifications due to signal averaging." ● Zawodny & Haskin (NASA LSAWT): a >5 dB directivity error at one elevation at BPF = 160 Hz "may be due to reflections from the wall-mounted wedges" — the array sat 0.49 m from the wedge tips, and "treating this as a 1/4-wavelength yields a notional cut-on frequency of 175 Hz", below the room's nominal 200 Hz. ●

03Qualification — why the verdict depends on the method

Three families of choice change a pass/fail verdict on the same raw data.

How you sample. Discrete 0.3 m steps versus a continuous traverse (Cunefare); ISO 26101 now demands ≤ λ/10 spacing below 1 kHz and 25 mm above (Winker). Jenny's ultrasonic case is the reductio: at 63 kHz, 5 cm steps are 9.2 wavelengths apart and "up and down swings in the data" are all that survive.

How you fit. ISO 3745 Annex A fits a/(r − r₀) with a free acoustic-centre offset; ISO 26101 fixes the slope at −20 lg(r/r₀) and only fits the level. Cunefare's Table IX: the optimal-reference fit cut the out-of-tolerance fraction at 80 Hz from 56 % to 13 % on identical data. NPL: a free-r₀ regression passed a room with no wedges at all; with the draft limit r₀ < half the source size, broadband "could not be achieved at any frequency even for room state 1". Winker: ISO 3745 only says r₀ "should" fall within 200 mm. The two standards "do not always agree on the verdict" (Russo, Euronoise 2018).

What signal. Tones or band noise — §1. ISO 3745:2012 made tones primary; ISO 26101 allows both.

Geometry rules. ISO 26101:2012 required traversing to λ/2 of the lowest band, which Winker shows limits a 3.35 × 3.05 × 2.74 m chamber to 214 Hz "due to geometry constraints"; the 2017 revision relaxed it to λ/4, and the University of Split chamber (2.8 × 1.7 × 2.05 m) only reached 250 Hz by moving the source to one wall. Near field: start the fit no closer than r = max(0.5, 95/f) m from (kr)² > 3 — at 80 Hz, starting at 0.5 m "shows a strong near-field impact" (Cunefare Fig. 17).

Uncertainty consequence. NPL translated room quality into sound-power error: rooms that qualify under the 1977 broadband rule can carry A-weighted errors of 6.4–7.3 dB (standard uncertainty ~4 dB), against ~0.02 dB for rooms that qualify on tones. Their conclusion: "the procedure for qualifying rooms using the broad-band noise methodology is over-tolerant of poor acoustic room performance."

It is our opinion that anechoic chambers should be tested with pure tones and a continuous traversing system. The counter-argument, that such traversing systems or that discrete sampling on a fine spacing is impractical, is specious: if the intent is to find where the performance deviates from inverse square law, then the physics demand such a spacing.Cunefare et al., JASA 113(2), 2003, p. 892 ●

04What people do about it

a · Design side

  • Depth sets the cut-off; material shifts it less. Wang & Tang (BEM, 1996): cut-off "strongly affected by the length of the wedge and the flow resistance"; base and air-gap thickness barely move it. Bonfiglio & Pompoli: the λ/4 rule "is not always respected" — above 10 kPa·s/m² a 100 Hz wedge needs > 85 cm. ●
  • Truncation is cheap. Cutting 15 % off the tip (12 cm) keeps α > 0.99 (Bonfiglio); 10–20 cm "changes little", 30 cm pushes |R| to 0.1 (Wang & Tang). ●
  • Hybrid and flat-wall linings. Two-density hybrid wedges lower the cut-off in the same depth (Wang & Tang); Jiang's uniform-then-gradient flat wall beats the wedge by 5 % depth at 100 Hz and gives a lower effective cut-off (120 vs 128 Hz), realisable as 10 discrete layers. ●
  • Mounting changes the answer. Schneider: impedance-tube data ignoring the wire frame "does not reflect the behavior of the acoustic lining in the chamber"; with melamine, frame motion matters. ●
  • Predicting the chamber cut-off, not the wedge cut-off. Singh, Garg & Narayanan replace c/(4L) with f = 2.25c/(d − l_w − a), d being the source-to-wall distance, l_w the wedge length, a the source size: 312–320 Hz predicted against 315 Hz measured, "within 3%". Validated on one chamber only, their own; c/(4L) under-predicted it by 10.5 %. ●
  • Design margin. Jiang: "a thorough simulation before real construction is necessary"; expect the effective cut-off 20–30 % above the wedge design value. ●

b · Placement

  • Source "as far as possible from the walls" (Schneider). Free field survives below cut-off "in a reduced volume of the chamber up to a distance of around 1 m from the boundaries" (Bonfiglio). ●
  • Microphones: c/(4 d_tip) per microphone (Zawodny); away from corners below 250 Hz (Rizzi); 1–1.5 m off the jet axis to stay out of the shear layer (Merino-Martínez). ●
  • Cover the nozzle of an open-jet tunnel for purely acoustic runs — the settling chamber reverberates (T60 = 0.22 s at Delft). ●

c · Signal and analysis

  • Qualify with tones; report the envelope (frequency × distance × direction), not "anechoic".
  • Spatial averaging: NPL rotated the hemisphere array four times (37 positions) — but the standard deviation across rotations (< 0.5 dB) "does not reveal room influence"; only the absolute offset between rooms does. ●
  • Environmental correction K₂ from a reference sound source: A-weighted sound-power error fell from 6.4/7.3 dB to 0.8/0.1 dB in NPL's degraded rooms; they propose K₂ < 0.5 dB as a hemi-anechoic criterion. Applies to sound power, not to directivity. ●
  • Time gating works where the reflection arrives late relative to the wavelength: Bahr gates laser-plasma pulses with a 600 µs Tukey window; Friot & Gintz report that at low frequency windowing "failed, mainly because … echoes from a scattering body are very difficult to separate from actuator responses", and Stroud's notch-EQ trick (○) exists precisely because the source's own decay outlasts the room. ●

d · Ground plane (hemi-anechoic rooms, outdoor plates)

  • Full anechoic configuration for spectra where it exists (Ma 2022). ●
  • Foam under the source: Alkmim covered 2.5 × 2.5 m with 0.07 m foam — reflection "minimized … but not fully suppressed, especially at low frequencies". ●
  • Porous ground treatment: Jawahar et al. cut sideline levels ~13 dB in 1.5–10 kHz and the OASPL increment by ≈ 2 dBA with 24 mm 75 PPI foam; even at four radii clearance the bare plate adds 4–8 dB above 1 kHz at shallow angles, absent at 90°. Angle-dependence with unchanged thrust = reflection; thrust-scaled change with new harmonics = aerodynamic ground effect. ●
  • Flush-mount the microphone in a ground board: the reflection "becomes well defined and will result in a simple pressure doubling" (Rasmussen). A pole microphone over any plane is the worst case for a hovering source. ●

e · Recirculation (closed rooms, rotors)

  • Quiescent air: ≥ 5 min settle (Ma 2024, Zawodny); fast ramp-up (3.5 s) to maximise the clean window (Weitsman). ●
  • Downstream meshes at 3.3 D and 6 D: clean window 3.9 → ~10 s, random SPL uncertainty ±1.1 → ±0.5 dB; upstream or ducted meshes distort the inflow and make things worse (Weitsman). Not transferable: no benefit at Virginia Tech (Whelchel). ●
  • Geometry: wake into a discharge room (Gallo, VKI) or a diffuser (LSAWT); ~6 D floor clearance and > 12 D to the wall along the wake showed no onset in 30 s (Fasulo). ●
  • Always record a spectrogram from spin-up and report which window was used.

f · Modelling the room (before building, or before trusting)

  • Complex-image-source sum with a spherical-wave oblique reflection coefficient (Bonfiglio & Pompoli) reproduces measured decay at the cut-off in 650–800 m³ rooms; Jiang's modification with FEM-derived oblique wedge impedance improves it "in the critical region, where the 1.5 dB deviation is violated". ●
  • Locally-reacting admittance from a tube "failed to predict the quality of the chambers at frequencies below ~150 Hz, regardless of the admittance applied"; three linings with α = 0.997/1.0/0.997 give different free-field regions — α alone is not a design index at low frequency (Schneider). ●
  • Geometrical (ray) models carry energy, not phase, and cannot represent the failure mode at all (see the parent review, §12). Room-acoustic round robins put simulation-vs-measurement spread at several dB even with fixed input data (Brinkmann 2019 ●; Vorländer 2013 ●).

g · Active absorption below the passive limit

  • Haasjes: Kirchhoff–Helmholtz integral on a ring of pressure + velocity sensors isolates the reflected field; feedforward FIR control gave 13–15 dB reduction numerically and 9.4–9.6 dB in a real-time 2-D rig up to ~600 Hz, with T10 dropping from 0.036 to 0.016–0.026 s. Sensor spacing must be ≤ c/(2 f_max). ●
  • Friot & Gintz: ~10 dB reduction of scattered pressure at 280 Hz in the LMA room; scaling to 100 Hz in 10 × 7 × 6 m needs ~3 transducers per wavelength, i.e. ~100 microphones and loudspeakers, and a feedforward reference that "might be problematic for … aeroacoustic noise sources". Post-processing subtraction of the estimated scattered field is the fallback. ●
  • Ma et al. (Applied Acoustics 2022): a loudspeaker inside a large micro-perforated-panel cavity drives absorption "nearly close to 1" up to ~400 Hz for a 0.6 × 0.8 m panel, but only below the first cavity mode the source can excite (215–430 Hz depending on placement; "the larger the size … the narrower the controllable frequency band"); antisymmetric modes contribute nothing, so the source belongs on their nodal lines at the cavity centre. ●

05What this means for the arc rig

Our situation, read through the table above

Six microphones on a vertical arc, one elevation each, a rotor on a Tyto 1585 stand, tonal sources with BPF in the 150–300 Hz band for two-blade props at 4500–9000 rpm — exactly the band where every chamber in this set is weakest, and exactly the signal type that band-noise qualification cannot see. Fasulo's CIRA study uses the same stand in a 90 Hz semi-anechoic room and reports floor-filled nulls, 4 dB pylon shading and decay ripples. The arc turns a room error into a directivity error directly: a reflection that lifts one elevation by 3 dB is indistinguishable, after the fact, from a lobe.

before any campaign
Tonal draw-away at BPF and 2–3 × BPF along each microphone's radial with the stand and arc in place; plot against a fixed −6 dB/doubling line; record r₀ from the ISO 3745 fit and reject the position if it is implausible.
per microphone
Compute c/(4 d_tip); flag every band below it as trend-only. Keep microphones off corners below 250 Hz.
per run
Spectrogram from spin-up; report the analysis window; test for harmonic growth at the out-of-plane microphones (BPF unchanged, harmonics ≥ 2 rising = recirculation). Settle ≥ 5 min between powered runs.
symmetry check
Repeat one configuration with reversed rotation sense; asymmetry that flips is the room or the stand, not the prop.
hemi-anechoic or floor plate
Adjacent-microphone comparison near the floor; foam under the source; never a pole microphone over a hard plane for a tonal source without stating the comb frequencies c/(4h), c/(2h) in the report.
reporting
State the qualified envelope (Hz × m × direction) and the signal used. "Measured in an anechoic chamber" without it is an assumption.
