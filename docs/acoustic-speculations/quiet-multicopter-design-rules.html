Quiet Multicopter Design Rules

What the published record says about building a multicopter that makes less noise: where the rotors go relative to each other and to the arms, how the blades are shaped, when a duct helps, and what linings can and cannot do. Every number here is someone else's, with the conditions it was measured under.

00The rules, before the evidence

In short Most of a small multicopter's avoidable noise comes from things passing too close to the blades — an arm, another rotor, a strut, a duct lip — and from spinning faster than the thrust needs. The airframe rules are the cheap ones and the best measured. Duct and lining rules are mostly aerodynamic; the acoustic evidence for ducts is thin and often says they make a static rotor louder.
  1. Rotor above the arm, arm well below the tip plane. On a single rotor with a chord-wide arm, moving the arm from 0.1 to 0.2 R below the tip gave the biggest step (up to ~8 dB tonal, ~10 dBA at the lowest microphone); by 0.4 R the tones were back to the isolated rotor [E]. A full-quadcopter simulation put rotors mounted under the arms 8.1 dB louder than rotors above them [S].
  2. In forward flight, keep the rear rotors out of the front wakes. Raising the rotors 0.33 R above the airframe on standoffs cut a real quadcopter's A-weighted level by 4–8 dBA at −10° pitch [E]. The noise came from the wake the front blade sheds as it passes over its own strut junction, not just from the front rotor.
  3. Side-by-side spacing has no measured noise rule. The best available is a simulation: interaction fades out by a tip gap of 0.75–1.0 D, and costs 6–8 % thrust at 0.2–0.3 D [S]. Commercial quadcopters sit at roughly 0.34–0.46 D. Going from two to four rotors on a Phantom added 6–8 dB of broadband where one to two added 3 dB [E].
  4. Coaxial pairs: axial gap 0.2–0.4 D. Agreed across several independent experiments; below 0.2 D more blades make it worse [E].
  5. If you can control rotor phase, do. Holding two adjacent propellers 90° apart cut the first blade tone by 19 dB, and once phase was controlled the spacing hardly mattered [E].
  6. Make the thrust at lower tip speed. Every route that lets the rotor turn slower for the same thrust helps: a twisted blade (+9.3 % figure of merit and up to −4.3 dB [E]), more blades at constant thrust, higher solidity. At equal tip speed, more blades are louder in broadband.
  7. Edge treatments work under conditions, and manufacturing sets the sign. A boundary-layer trip kills the laminar tones but costs 3–11 % thrust [E]. Trailing-edge serrations need tooth height > 0.25 of the boundary layer, angle < 45° and St > 1; 3D-printed cut-in serrations with a blunt root made noise worse [E].
  8. A duct is an aerodynamic device first. Tip gap dominates its thrust gain, and the literature puts a cliff between about 1 % and 3.6 % of radius [S]. Acoustically, a static 30 cm duct raised noise everywhere on the array, by up to 12 dB, and only helped with 10 m/s inflow [E].
  9. At drone thickness, only resonant liners reach the blade tone. No passive absorber can be flat-broadband from ~400 Hz in 10–30 mm [T]; a 30 mm porous layer peaks near 2.8 kHz. Thin tuned liners have cut real fan tones by 3–11 dB(A) [E].
  10. Judge with what people hear, not dB alone. A redesigned propeller changed the plain level by under 1.8 dB yet cut modelled psychoacoustic annoyance by up to 20 % [E]. Drones at 5.6 dB lower exposure level were rated as annoying as road vehicles [R].

06What this means for our two research trees

These are our readings of the rules above against the questions in duct-research-tree and drone-paczek-research-tree. They are predictions to test, not results.

Duct tree (6-in propeller, R ≈ 76 mm)

  • Tip gap 1 vs 3 mm is 1.3 % vs 3.9 % of radius. That straddles the drop the 5-in CFD put between 1.1 % and 3.6 %. Expect the 3 mm duct to lose most of the thrust gain. The acoustic literature, thin as it is, says the tip gap matters less for noise than whether the lip flow separates.
  • Duct height 25/50/100 mm. Length is aerodynamically second-order. The acoustic lever is centring the propeller axially, which a taller duct makes possible. Separately, a thickness of ≥ 15 mm at the prop plane (10 % of D) is the cited guard against static separation.
  • Rod count. The only evidence is fenestron-scale: move struts away from the rotor plane (5.5 % Dt gave up to 10 dB). Rod count sets which rotor–strut tones appear (Tyler–Sofrin, per the team review).
  • Test static, compare at equal thrust. A static test may well show the duct louder (Malgoezar), so report thrust with every capture.
  • Liner placement. Inside at the tip is where a liner has the most effect, and where it costs the most thrust unless it is buried in grooves. 25 mm porous liners will not touch the 200–670 Hz blade tone; a resonant liner might.
  • Motor spacing. This is side-by-side rotor spacing, which has no measured noise rule. A spacing sweep on our rig would be new data.

Parcel-drone tree (enclosure linings)

  • No paper found measures EPP, XPS, cardboard or felt acoustically. Our dp1 captures are the only data on them we know of.
  • At tens of millimetres, expect every porous lining to do little below ~500 Hz, and differences to show above ~1 kHz. Cork and Thinsulate literature agrees.
  • If the propeller sweep finds a strong blade tone, only a tuned resonator in the lining can target it. Seams and leaks can cost 10 dB of the effect.
  • Rank materials with loudness and sharpness as well as dB(A), since a lining that removes high-frequency hiss may matter more to listeners than its level change suggests.

On our own arc

Layout effects of 3–8 dB are within what the arc can resolve in broadband, but blade tones below ~1 kHz are still provisional on it, and the hub sphere is not axisymmetric at 3–5 kHz. Treat arc results on tones with the same caution as arc-rig-onepager.pdf.

07Where the sources disagree, and what nobody has measured

  • Toroidal propellers: a careful 5-in test found one louder by up to 10 dB; other studies report 5–20 dB reductions. Methods differ widely.
  • Cost of tripping: a thin tape trip reported negligible loss; grit and rough-surface trips measured 3–11 % thrust. The trip type decides it.
  • Serrations: clean reductions on machined blades, increases on printed cut-in blades. Say how it was made.
  • Diffuser angle: no effect, a negative effect, and a positive-then-negative effect in three historical studies. Each held a different parameter fixed.
  • Tip speed: at fixed blade shape, lower tip speed is quieter; across blade shapes, a quieter optimised blade once spun at twice the rpm (Sinibaldi 2012).
  • Coaxial optimum and size: 0.2–0.4 D for most studies, 0.1 D for one 14-in rotor.
  • Not found anywhere:
    • a measured noise rule for side-by-side rotor spacing on a vehicle with arms;
    • round vs rectangular vs aerofoil arm sections;
    • tandem-rotor spacing;
    • landing-gear noise on small multirotors;
    • leading-edge tubercles or coatings on small propellers;
    • any acoustic data on EPP, XPS, cardboard or felt;
    • a per-propeller vs whole-drone duct comparison with both thrust and noise.
