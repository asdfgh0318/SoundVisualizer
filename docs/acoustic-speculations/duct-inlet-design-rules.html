Duct Inlet Design Rules

How to shape the inlet of a static ducted propeller so the duct adds thrust instead of costing it: tip gap, lip radius, wall thickness, where the propeller sits, and the outlet. Written for the APC 8x4.5MR duct of the 2026-10-27 research tree. Every number in the rules is someone else's, with the conditions it was measured under.

In short Close the blade-tip gap to about 1 mm first; then give the inlet a generously rounded lip (10–13 % of the throat diameter, about 20–26 mm here) on a wall at least 20 mm thick, with the propeller set back behind the lip. The outlet flare is second-order. On a duct this small, getting the gap and the lip wrong turns the duct from a thrust gain into a thrust loss — which is what our unlined duct measures today.

The passages the first two rules stand on

“While the tip clearances of 0.6% and 1.1% produce comparable results, a sudden drop in performance can be observed for 3.6% tip clearance.”Chew, Gan & Hesse, AIAA SciTech 2021, p. 14
“increasing the tip clearance from 3.04% to 5.17% of the rotor radius can lead to a significant reduction in hover efficiency of up to 18.1%, even falling below the performance of an open rotor configuration.”Dayhoum, Ramirez-Serrano & Martinuzzi, Appl. Sci. 15 (2025) 9594, p. 9
“As the tip gap is increased, the suction pressure near the leading edge rapidly collapses”Martin & Tung, AHS 60th Forum 2004, p. 3
“Increasing the lip radius and decreasing the blade tip clearance caused performance to improve, while optimal values of diffuser angle and length were found to be 10° and 50% of the shroud throat diameter, respectively.”Pereira, PhD dissertation, Univ. of Maryland 2008, p. 1

03What a good duct can give — and why a small one can lose

“Compared to the baseline open rotor, the shrouded rotors showed increases in thrust by up to 94%, at the same power consumption, or reductions in power by up to 62% at the same thrust.”Pereira 2008, p. 1 — 16 cm rotor, best of 17 shrouds
“the duct thrust developed to approximately 38% of the isolated rotor thriist [sic] at high RPM.”Martin & Tung 2004, p. 3 — 10-inch rotor, smallest tip gap (1 % R)
“The internal flow losses tend to counteract the duct thrust, and beiow [sic] a certain RPM the duct produces a net download (compared to the isolated rotor).”Martin & Tung 2004, p. 3
“for a given overall aircraft size limitation, and ignoring considerations of the safety benefits of a shroud, a larger-diameter open rotor is more likely to give better performance than a smaller-diameter shrouded rotor.”Pereira 2008, p. 2

So the duct's thrust is suction on the inlet lip: a wide gap or a sharp lip kills it, and at small scale the viscous losses inside the duct can then outweigh what is left. Our unlined duct (§01) sits on the losing side. The gain is not scale-free either: over a similar tip-clearance range Hubbard's 48-inch model lost ~84 % of its thrust and Mort's 7-foot fan ~20 % (Pereira 2008, p. 70, 74), so no percentage here transfers to an 8-inch duct without measuring it.

04Where the sources disagree

  • Diffuser angle. Pereira finds 10° best; Taylor 1958 finds the result “virtually independent of the expansion angle of the diffuser, and depended only on the value of the expansion ratio” (Pereira p. 74); Dyer found “increasing the diffuser angle caused thrust and power loading to decrease, contrary to predictions” (Pereira p. 94); Li, Yonezawa & Liu's CFD put the optimum at 0° (Drones 2021, p. 10). Keep the flare modest and treat it as a later experiment.
  • Diffuser length. Pereira gives a single optimum (31–50 % Dt); Ma’arof 2023 found no correlation with thrust and named tip clearance the most consistent driver (p. 314).
  • How small the gap must be. On 30-inch propellers a gap of 0.12→0.56 % Dt cost under 5 % thrust (Pereira p. 79), while 0→4.4 % Dt cost Hubbard ~84 % (p. 70): the penalty grows once the gap is already a few percent, which is where a 3–5 mm gap on our duct would be.
  • Lip radius against crosswind. The larger lip gives more static thrust; Martin & Tung's smaller lip “improved the stability for hover in a crosswind” (p. 4). On the stand only the first matters.

05How to test it on our rig

  1. Record the duct first: inner diameter, tip gap, duct length, where the propeller disc sits along it, and the present inlet edge (sharp, chamfered or rounded). Without these the millimetres in §02 are guesses.
  2. One change at a time, against same-day references. Each session: bare propeller and unlined duct at the start (and the bare propeller again at the end), then the variant; repeats of one set-up agree within 0.1 N, so differences above ~0.2 N are real.
  3. Order: (a) a bolt-on rounded lip ring, ~25 mm radius, on the existing duct; (b) a tighter tip gap (the linings already show the direction); (c) only then the outlet flare.
  4. Compare at equal rpm or per watt, not only at equal ESC signal: the bare propeller ran 4 % slower than the ducted one, and the NASA rig needed 4.6 % more rpm with the duct to match the open propeller's thrust (Zawodny 2025, p. 49).
  5. Photograph every set-up.
