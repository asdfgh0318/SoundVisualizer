Where the Reflection Came From

A divergence-loss test tells you a chamber is failing. It does not tell you which surface is doing it. This is the ladder of methods that does — and what each rung actually costs.

Scope acoustic, diagnostic Date 1 Sep 2026 Companion to Absorptive But Not Anechoic Library papers/reflection-localization/

The qualification standard gives you a number per traverse direction: this room deviates from the inverse square law by so many decibels, along this line, at this frequency. That is a detector, not a locator. Five traverses over a whole room is a spatial resolution of about a fifth of a sphere, and the answer it returns — "something reflects, somewhere over there" — is not enough to act on. You cannot treat a direction.

Locating the reflector is a separate measurement with separate instruments, and it is the step between knowing you have a problem and fixing it. The good news is that the cheap rungs of the ladder are very cheap, the expensive rungs are genuinely expensive, and the decisive rung — the one that proves you were right — is a moving blanket.

01

The ladder

Ordered the way you would actually climb it. Most problems are solved by tier 1 plus tier 5; tiers 2–4 exist for when the reflectogram shows several arrivals and you cannot guess which surface is which.

TIER 0

Divergence-loss traverse — ISO 26101-1

The qualification test itself. Sound pressure against distance along five or more straight paths, compared to a fitted inverse-square line. This is where you find out you have a problem at all.

Gives that it fails, at which frequencies, and along which of ~5 directions.
Costs a source, a microphone, a traverse rig.
Misses where. Entirely.
TIER 1

Impulse response and reflectogram

A swept sine through a small loudspeaker at the source position, deconvolved to an impulse response. Every discrete reflection is a peak; its delay after the direct sound converts straight to excess path length, Δs = c·τ. Strength comes free in the same plot. This is the first thing to do and it answers most questions.

Gives how many reflections, how late, how strong, and the usable gate length.
Costs one microphone, one loudspeaker, free software.
Misses direction — a 4.7 ms arrival could be from any point on an ellipsoid.
TIER 2

Image-source trilateration

Repeat tier 1 at four or more known microphone positions. Each gives one range to the same image source; solve for its 3D position, and the reflecting plane is the perpendicular bisector between the real source and the image. No array hardware, no phase coherence — see §02.

Gives the reflector's position in 3D, over-determined and checkable.
Costs microphones you already own, plus surveying their positions.
Misses nothing geometric — but you must match peaks across channels.
TIER 3

Spherical array — time-gated DoA + ToA

An EigenMike-class array measures a spatial impulse response; beamforming gated around each arrival gives direction as well as time, which fixes the image source in one measurement and resolves the peak-matching problem automatically. This is the method the room-geometry literature converged on.

Gives full room geometry from one position. RMS boundary error 2.7–4.7 cm simulated, 11.5–18.9 cm measured.
Costs a 32-channel spherical array.
Misses above its aliasing frequency — 8 kHz for the EM32.
TIER 4

Spatial Decomposition Method, or an acoustic camera

SDM assigns a direction of arrival to every sample of the impulse response, producing a time-resolved map of image sources — this is how the loudspeaker-directivity study traced which surfaces were corrupting its polar plots. A conventional acoustic camera can do the job too, but reads backwards; see §04.

Gives a picture, which is what convinces people who were not in the room.
Costs a compact intensity array, or a camera rental.
Misses — has documented limits on resolving two simultaneous reflections.
TIER 5

The blanket test

Drape absorber over the surface you accused. Re-run tier 0. If the deviation goes away, you were right; if it does not, you were not. Everything above this rung produces a hypothesis. This is the only step that produces a fact.

Gives proof, and a preview of the fix.
Costs a blanket and twenty minutes.
Misses nothing. Do not skip it.

The gap between tier 0 and tier 5 is where most chamber problems actually die. Nash's 51 m³ chamber is a tier-0 result that was never carried further: the report names two plausible culprits — an expanded-metal floor grate and foam wedges that had never been impedance-tube tested — and neither was confirmed. That is not a criticism of the work; it is what a qualification test is scoped to deliver. It is just not the end of the job.

02

Tier 2 in detail — the rung most people skip

It needs no array, no phase coherence between channels, and no equipment you do not already have. It is the best value on the ladder and it is worth understanding why it works.

The whole calculation

for each microphone i at known position Mᵢ: dᵢ = |S − Mᵢ| direct path, from geometry τᵢ = t_reflection − t_direct read off channel i's impulse response Rᵢ = dᵢ + c·τᵢ range from the image source solve |S′ − Mᵢ| = Rᵢ for S′ (≥4 mics; subtract pairs → linear least squares) n = (S′ − S) / |S′ − S| reflector plane normal x₀ = (S + S′) / 2 a point on the plane Pᵢ = where the segment S′→Mᵢ crosses that plane — the actual bounce point

The one hard part

Peak association, not geometry

Deciding that the third peak at M₁ and the third peak at M₄ are the same reflection is the step that breaks. With one strong discrete reflection it is trivial. With six surfaces and second-order arrivals interleaving, it is a combinatorial problem, and picking wrong puts S′ somewhere meaningless with no warning. This is the entire reason the literature pays for spherical arrays: direction of arrival resolves the association that timing alone cannot. Practical mitigation — start with the strongest single arrival, confirm it with tier 5, treat it, and repeat.

03

What this looks like on the arc rig

Two results fall straight out of the geometry, and one of them is a problem nobody has looked at yet.

Settled — do not plan around it

The UMIK arc cannot beamform, and it is not close

server/core/trigger_sync.py aligns channels on an RMS block onset with block_size = 128. At 48 kHz that is a 2.67 ms alignment quantum — 91 cm of sound path. Beamforming needs inter-channel timing good to tens of microseconds. Even granting perfect sync, eleven microphones at 18° on a radius-r arc have chord spacing 0.313·r, which spatially aliases above 548 Hz at r = 1 m. It is not an acoustic camera, it cannot be made into one, and no amount of software fixes either number.

Available today

The same eleven microphones are an excellent tier-2 array

Because τᵢ never crosses channels, the 2.67 ms alignment quantum is irrelevant to it. Within one channel the resolution is one sample: 20.8 µs, or 7.1 mm of path. Eleven microphones at surveyed positions give eleven ranges to each image source, against three unknowns — heavily over-determined, so the residual tells you whether your peak association was right. The rig you built for directivity is, unmodified, a reflection locator.

This is the third instance of the same structural pattern: a room artefact that correlates with the independent variable rather than scattering randomly across it. Recirculation correlates with PWM because the run ramps monotonically; chamber deviation correlates with direction because chambers are anisotropic; and now the floor-bounce gate correlates with elevation because the arc is vertical. None of the three is noise, and averaging does not touch any of them.

The numbers above are computed from an assumed 1.0 m arc radius and 1.2 m rotor height. Both are unknown — the radius is still the blocking input — but the shape of the curve does not depend on them. Only its position does.

04

Acoustic cameras, read correctly

They do work for this. They just do not show you what a first-time user expects.

A conventional planar acoustic camera images sources. Point one into a chamber whose wall is reflecting and it will paint a hotspot — but that hotspot is S′ from the figure above, the image source, sitting behind the wall rather than on it. Practitioner guidance for acoustic imaging treats this as the canonical false positive, with a simple field test: move the array and re-scan. A real source stays put; a reflection shifts or disappears, because its apparent position is tied to the array's viewpoint through the mirror geometry.

So the camera is a locator, provided you read the ghost as the image source and mirror it back. What it is not is a turnkey answer, and a plain broadband scan of a running rotor will not give you one — you need the arrival gated in time, which means an impulse response, which means the swept sine again.

The instrument the research literature actually converged on is a spherical array rather than a planar camera, because it resolves direction over the full sphere and pairs naturally with time-of-arrival. Lovedee-Turner and Murphy recover complete room geometry from a single EigenMike EM32 measurement, with the accuracy quoted in the ladder. The constraints are worth repeating: every boundary must be at least 50 cm from both source and array, the EM32 aliases above 8 kHz, and accuracy degrades sharply — 16 to 25 cm — once the room stops being convex.

05

What the standards say to do about it

There is a formal answer to “the room is not anechoic and I cannot fix it”, and it is not to keep adjusting the room until it passes.

ISO 26101 has a Part 2, published 2024, and it is the half of the standard that almost nobody cites. Part 1 asks whether a space is a free field. Part 2 qualifies a space that only approximates a free field near one or more reflecting planes, and quantifies what is left over as the environmental correction K₂ — the term that corrects measured sound pressure for reflected sound when deriving sound power. In an ideal free field K₂ = 0; the standard's job is to tell you how far from zero you actually are, and it notes that K₂ generally grows with the area of the measurement surface.

That reframes the problem usefully. You are not obliged to achieve an anechoic room. You are obliged to measure how far from one you are, report it, and correct for it.

For a rotor on a stand in an ordinary room, clause 5 is the one to reach for: a calibrated reference sound source is a modest purchase, it works without removing the rig, and it gives K₂ in frequency bands rather than a single broadband number. It also composes with everything above — the same reference source drives the tier-1 impulse response.

06

The procedure, end to end

One afternoon for steps 1–5 with hardware already on the bench.

  1. Put a loudspeaker where the rotor goes. Small, reasonably omnidirectional. The rotor cannot give you an impulse response; a swept sine can. Survey its acoustic centre and every microphone position — tier 2's accuracy is now geometry-limited, not timing-limited, so the tape measure is the instrument that matters.
  2. Record an exponential sine sweep on all channels with trigger.enabled = false. Onset alignment shifts the time origin you are about to measure delays from. Same trap as the recirculation take.
  3. Deconvolve to impulse responses and plot each as an energy-time curve. Read off the direct sound, then every discrete peak and its delay. You now have the gate length for every microphone — and, as §03 shows, they will not be the same.
  4. Convert delays to path lengths — Δs = c·τ — and sanity-check them against the room you can see. A 1.6 m excess at the 0° mic in a 1.2 m-high rig is the floor, and you should be able to predict it before measuring it. If a peak has no candidate surface, that is the interesting one.
  5. Trilaterate the strongest unexplained arrival across four or more channels, per §02. Check the residual: a good association gives a consistent S′, a bad one does not converge.
  6. Blanket the surface you identified and re-run tier 0. Deviation gone means solved. Deviation unchanged means the association was wrong — go back to step 5 with the next arrival.
  7. If several arrivals interleave and step 5 will not converge, that is the signal to escalate to tier 3 or 4 — hire a spherical array for a day rather than buying one. That is the point where direction of arrival earns its cost, and not before.
  8. Whatever remains, quantify it. Determine K₂ by ISO 26101-2 clause 5 with a reference sound source and report it alongside the results. A stated correction is a defensible measurement; an unstated reflection is not.

The habit worth keeping

Predict the reflection before you measure it

Every surface you can see has a computable arrival time. Working them out first turns the reflectogram from a puzzle into a checklist: floor at 4.7 ms, ceiling at 9 ms, the equipment rack at 6 ms. The peaks that match are confirmations; the peaks that do not are the actual findings. This costs five minutes and removes most of the peak-association problem before it starts.
