Absorptive But Not Anechoic

Simulating an anechoic chamber before you trust it: what the models actually predict, which failures they catch, and the twelve ways a room that looks fine turns out not to be one.

Scope acoustic only Date 1 Sep 2026 Retrieved 21 sources Paywalled 9 papers Library papers/anechoic-simulation/

In 2019 Anthony Nash qualified a 51 m³ IAC anechoic chamber against Annex A of ISO 3745. Driven with pink noise in one-third-octave bands, it passed. Driven with pure tones at the same band centres, it failed. One tone at 2056 Hz fitted a decay of −26.4 dB per decade of distance — about 8 dB per doubling, a rate that is not physically possible in a free field and can only come from a reflection cancelling the direct wave. The chamber had exposed foam wedges on all six surfaces, an expanded-metal floor grate, and no evidence that its wedges had ever been tested in an impedance tube.

That is the failure simulation exists to find, and the reason "will it be quiet?" is the wrong question. A room lined with foam is always quiet. The question is whether the field inside it falls off like 1/r along the directions you actually measure, at the frequencies you care about, at the distances your microphones actually sit at. No chamber has a single answer to that. It has an answer per direction, per distance, per frequency, per source position — and simulation is how you find the shape of that envelope before you own the room, or before you publish numbers taken inside one you already have.

In short, the chamber is absorptive but not anechoic. A. Nash, Qualification of an anechoic chamber, ICA 2019 — a chamber that passed on noise and failed on tones
01

“Simulating a chamber” is three different jobs

They answer different questions, cost different amounts, and fail in different places. Most published work does the first and third properly and takes a shortcut through the second.

The distinction matters because the three stages are usually conflated. "We simulated the chamber" most often means stage 1 — an absorption coefficient for one wedge — and stage 1 tells you almost nothing about whether the room qualifies. A wedge with α = 0.99 at normal incidence sits in a room where nearly every ray arrives obliquely, off a boundary that is not locally reacting, from a source radiating spherical rather than plane waves. Each of those three gaps has its own literature.

The scale in the FEM row is worth internalising before budgeting a room simulation. A published domain-decomposition run modelled a 2271 m³ auditorium up to 3 kHz with roughly 150 million degrees of freedom, on 512 CPU cores, taking about 2.5 hours for a 3-second impulse response. A small chamber is orders of magnitude cheaper — but the scaling is cubic in frequency, so the cost of going from 3 kHz to 12 kHz is a factor of 64, not 4. This is exactly why nobody meshes a whole chamber to 20 kHz, and why the practical answer is a wave solver at low frequency where the chamber actually fails, and an impedance-based image-source model everywhere else.

03

Twelve ways a chamber lies to you

Every one of these is documented, and every one is something a simulation can be built to expose before you build or before you publish. ● read at source · ○ abstract or secondary report only.

01 — cut-off

Cut-off is set by absorber depth, not by absorber quality

The lowest frequency of high absorption is roughly where the wedge length equals a quarter wavelength. A better material inside the same depth does not move it much; a deeper structure does. Below the cut-off, level versus distance goes flat or steep and the polar shape compresses.

● Beranek & Sleeper 1946: 40 Hz needs 94 in from tip to backing wall. ○ Singh et al. 2020 fit an empirical cut-off from source volume, chamber volume and wedge height to within ±3% of measurement.

02 — geometry

The geometry carries the absorption, not the material

Reading α off a flat sample of the lining tells you very little about how the lining performs once it is cut into wedges or pyramids. The difference is not a correction factor; it is the whole effect.

● ITMO chamber: a 25 mm disk cut from the lining measures α < 0.5 across 290–1800 Hz; the pyramid made of the same material simulates at α = 0.815 at 290 Hz rising past 0.9 above 410 Hz and reaching 0.996.

03 — boundaries

The locally-reacting assumption is wrong for porous wedges

Nearly every room simulation assumes normal velocity at a surface depends only on local pressure. Porous absorbers are extended-reaction: their impedance depends on incidence angle, and sound propagates inside the material. Modelling them as a local admittance is the standard shortcut and the standard source of optimism.

● Prinn, A Review of FEM for Room Acoustics (2023), §6.3.3: extended-reaction models give lower error than locally-reacting ones, verified experimentally.

04 — input data

Your single input parameter is ambiguous before you start

Fit the same impedance-tube curve with five standard empirical models and you get five different flow resistivities. Pick one and you have silently chosen an answer. Carry the spread and you have an uncertainty band you can actually defend.

● ITMO Table 1, same sample: Delany–Bazley 3437, Mechel 5136, Miki 5994, Garai–Pompoli 6125, JCA 8701 Pa·s/m².

05 — test signal

Tones expose what band noise hides

The single most important result in this literature. One-third-octave noise averages over the interference pattern; a tone sits in it. A chamber can pass a broadband qualification and fail a tonal one at the same band centres — and drone noise is tonal, dominated by blade-pass frequency and its harmonics.

● Nash 2019: passed on pink noise; on tones, 2056 Hz fitted −26.4 dB/decade (R²=0.3), 1600 Hz fitted −10.0 (R²=0.5), against an ideal of −20.

06 — anisotropy

A chamber is not anechoic; it is anechoic in an envelope

Qualification is per source position, per direction, per distance, per frequency. Publishing "measured in an anechoic chamber" without that envelope is publishing an assumption. The envelope is usually much smaller than the room.

● ITMO: qualifies 50–3150 Hz at 0.5–0.9 m in every direction, but the four directions diverge sharply beyond that; worst deviation found was 15.5 dB.

07 — discontinuities

Grates, ports, doors and recesses are the actual reflectors

The failures found in practice are rarely "the wedges are bad everywhere". They are one untreated surface producing a strong specular return at specific angles. ISO 26101-1 makes this explicit: traverses shall be aimed at boundary features with non-uniform treatment.

● Nash: expanded-metal walking grate, reflective at high frequency. ● ITMO: a 0.15 × 1.3 × 3.55 m floor recess left uncoated, plus flat ceiling patches instead of pyramids.

08 — fixtures

Your own rig is inside the sound field

Booms, turntables, stands, cable runs and the mount arm all scatter. This shows up as comb filtering and phantom lobes in the polar plot — indistinguishable, after the fact, from real directivity.

● ISO 26101-1 §5.1.3.2: "Sound reflection from the microphone support system should be carefully avoided." ● González & Kob (DAGA 2026) attribute measurable directivity error to turntable and nearby-surface reflections.

09 — the fit

The same raw traverse can pass or fail depending on how you fit it

ISO 3745 fits a/(r−r₀) with a fitted acoustic-centre offset r₀; ISO 26101 fits b − 20·log(r/r₀) with r₀ = 1 m fixed. These are different reference methods and they do not always agree on the verdict.

● Russo et al. (Euronoise 2018) set both equations side by side. ○ Cunefare et al. (JASA 2003) report that an optimal reference method "significantly improves the apparent performance of the chamber for pure tone qualifications".

10 — room size

A small chamber can be unqualifiable at low frequency by rule, not by physics

The traverse itself has a minimum length in wavelengths. ISO 26101:2012 demanded λ/2, which made small chambers impossible to qualify low; the 2017 revision relaxed it to λ/4. Chamber dimensions therefore set the lowest band you can claim, independent of how well the room performs.

● University of Split, 2.8 × 1.7 × 2.05 m: with the source centred, the lowest qualifiable third-octave band was 250 Hz — solved by moving the source to one wall and qualifying towards the opposite one.

11 — provenance

Nobody ever tested the wedges

Foam degrades, gets compressed, gets replaced with whatever was available. A purchased chamber is not evidence of a qualified chamber, and a chamber qualified once is not a chamber qualified now.

● Nash 2019, on a commercially manufactured chamber: "There was no evidence that the wedges were ever qualified by testing them in an impedance tube."

12 — the model

A ray-tracing model will tell you the chamber is fine

Geometrical acoustics carries energy, not phase. The chamber failure mode is coherent interference between the direct wave and a weak reflection — which an energy model cannot represent at all. It will return a clean, confident, wrong answer.

○ Round-robin studies on room-acoustic software report up to ±5 dB maximum deviation on sound strength and >50% differences on some quantities, with errors concentrated where modal density is low.

04

What the simulations themselves get wrong

Worth knowing before you spend a month on one, and before you quote its output in a thesis.

The input data is the error, not the solver. This is the consistent finding across the uncertainty literature. Wave-based methods solve the wave equation accurately; what they are given to solve is a set of boundary conditions derived from absorption coefficients measured under conditions that do not match the room. Vorländer's error-propagation analysis concludes that the input-data quality needed to predict reverberation time to within a just-noticeable difference is simply not available from reverberation-room measurements. Formal uncertainty-quantification studies in wave-based room acoustics target exactly three parameters: the flow resistivity of the porous absorber, its thickness, and the absorption of the remaining hard surfaces.

The diagnostic tell. When two different numerical methods agree with each other but both deviate from measurement, the material data is wrong, not the solver. That pattern is reported often enough to be treated as a rule.

A simulation can only fail the way you asked it to. This is the sharpest limitation and the one specific to this problem. Excite a chamber model with one-third-octave noise and you will faithfully reproduce Nash's passing result while the chamber is failing. The model was never wrong; the question was.

Validation exists and should be used. Benchmark databases for computational room acoustics are published and maintained — the sane order is to reproduce a benchmark case first, then model your own room, rather than trusting a first run.

The load-bearing conclusion

Simulation tells you where to point the microphone. It does not replace the traverse.

Nothing in this literature supports substituting a model for an ISO 26101-1 divergence-loss measurement. What the model buys is targeting: which frequencies, which directions, which distances are marginal, and therefore where the twenty measurements you can afford should go instead of the two hundred you cannot.

05

What this means for the arc rig

The relevant standard is ISO 5305:2024 — noise measurements for rotor-powered UAS under 150 kg — and its chamber clause is not generic.

ISO 5305 does not say "use an anechoic chamber". It says the inverse-square-law validity defined in ISO 26101-1 shall be ensured for all source positions and measurement directions specified in this document, with the test source placed in the positions closest to wall, floor and ceiling, and with microphone traverse directions along the measurement directions defined by the microphone positions. For an eleven-microphone elevation arc at 18° spacing that is up to eleven traverse directions, not the five generic ones in ISO 26101 Annex A.

The arc makes the room error worse, not neutral

A chamber's deviation is direction-dependent — that is the single most consistent empirical result in section 03. A conventional single-microphone measurement samples one row of that map and picks up an offset. An elevation arc samples eleven directions simultaneously, each with its own room error, and then the pipeline plots those eleven numbers as a polar shape. A direction-dependent room artefact therefore lands directly on the shape of the directivity plot, not just on the absolute level, and it will look exactly like real directivity.

This is structurally the same hazard the recirculation work identified — a room artefact correlated with the independent variable — but on the spatial axis instead of the time axis. It has the same consequence too: comparisons at a fixed elevation between two configurations are far better protected than the polar shape itself, because the room error is common-mode across configurations at that angle.

The cheapest useful simulation needs no chamber at all

If the answer to "is a chamber on the table?" turns out to be no, the fallback is time-gating, and the planning tool for it is the image-source method — a simulation, run on the back of an envelope. Two numbers govern it. The gate length tg sets the lowest trustworthy frequency at roughly 1/tg (ARTA's worked example: a 5.604 ms gate is valid above 178 Hz). And the image-source geometry says how high to mount everything: for a first floor reflection to arrive 5 ms after the direct sound at 0.48 m source–microphone spacing, source and microphone must sit at least 1.08 m above the floor. Rearranged for the arc geometry, that calculation gives the mounting height that buys a usable gate — and it is a five-line script, not a mesh.

One implementation note carried over from the recirculation work: any gating experiment must run with trigger.enabled = false. Trigger-onset alignment shifts the start of the signal, which corrupts the very time axis the gate is defined on.

06

The workflow I would run

Stage A is days of work on a laptop and answers the question in most cases. Stage B only happens if Stage A comes back marginal.

Stage A — impedance-driven, phase-preserving, no mesh

  1. Measure the lining in an impedance tube (ISO 10534-2, two-microphone method) on one flat sample. If there is no lining yet, do this for the candidate material. This is the only physical measurement Stage A needs.
  2. Fit flow resistivity with at least three models — Delany–Bazley, Miki, JCA — and keep the spread as an uncertainty band rather than picking a winner. Everything downstream inherits this band.
  3. FEM the actual wedge geometry in a unit cell: one wedge or pyramid in a square waveguide, Floquet periodicity on the side walls, PML at the far end, equivalent-fluid material inside. Output α(f, θ) and complex surface impedance. This is the step that separates "the material absorbs 0.4" from "the geometry absorbs 0.99".
  4. Build a phase-preserving image-source model of the room using those complex reflection coefficients. Source at the rotor position, receivers along all eleven arc directions. Include the untreated features explicitly — floor, door, any recess or grate — because they are what will fail.
  5. Run the virtual ISO 26101-1 divergence-loss test. Per direction, fit b − 20·log(r/r₀), compute ΔLp at each point, compare against the tolerance table. The output is the map in the second figure: frequency × distance × direction.
  6. Excite it with tones at one-third-octave centres, not band noise. If the simulated chamber fails only on tones, that is not a modelling artefact — that is the result, and it is the one that matters for blade-pass noise.
  7. Then measure. Run the real traverses along the directions the model flagged as marginal. The model chose where to spend the measurement time; it does not substitute for it.

Stage B — only if Stage A is marginal

Room FEM or FDTD below the crossover frequency, with extended-reaction absorber elements rather than a local admittance. Budget by f³: the mesh needs 6–10 degrees of freedom per wavelength, so doubling the top frequency costs eight times the degrees of freedom. Above the crossover, keep the image-source model. Reproduce a published benchmark case before trusting the first run on your own geometry.

Tooling

  • Stage A step 3 — COMSOL Pressure Acoustics, Frequency Domain is what the ITMO group used for exactly this unit cell, and it has Floquet periodicity and PML built in. The open-source route is Elmer, which solves cavity modes and the Helmholtz equation but needs more assembly for the periodic boundary.
  • Stage A step 4 — pyroomacoustics gives a fast image-source implementation that builds a real impulse response, so path-length interference and comb filtering do appear. What it does not give you is a frequency-dependent complex impedance per surface, which is precisely the thing stage 3 produced — so this step needs either a patched reflection coefficient or a hand-rolled image-source sum. It is roughly a hundred lines.
  • Alternative — openRay (a Gypsilab/MATLAB toolbox) and Just4RIR (C++ with a Blender front end) are GPL ray-tracing/image-source hybrids built for large meshes; useful if the room geometry is complicated, but they are energy-based and therefore subject to failure mode 12.
  • Stage A step 5 — no tooling needed. The fit and the tolerance comparison are the two equations quoted in section 03, card 09.

Evidence ledger

What was read at source, what was not, and what I need from you before stage 2.

Needed before the room can be evaluated

  • The arc radius. Not in config.toml, not in docs/mic_arc.md. It decides whether the existing 275 takes are far-field at all under R ≥ 5·DA.
  • Room dimensions and surfaces — L × W × H, floor material, what is on the walls, where the rig sits relative to them. This is the entire geometric input to Stage A step 4.
  • Mounting height of the rotor plane above the floor, and whether it can move.
  • Is a chamber actually on the table? Budget and space, or is this about characterising the room you already measure in? Stage A is the same either way; only what you do with the answer changes.
  • Prop diameters in the dp1 campaign, for the far-field and ground-clearance numbers.
