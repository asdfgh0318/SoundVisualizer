### Cunefare et al. 2003, JASA 113(2) 881–892 — Cunefare_2003_JASA_chamber-qualification-traverse-fit-signal
- Facility: Georgia Tech chamber, ~90 m³ inside wedge tips, perforated-metal wedges (42 % open area) with fiberglass lining, design range 80–10 000 Hz, wire-mesh floor 32 cm above floor wedge tips (p. 883).
- Tolerances (Table I, ISO 3745 / ANSI S12.35): ±1.5 dB <630 Hz, ±1.0 dB 800–5000 Hz, ±1.5 dB >6300 Hz.
- Problem: the deviation from inverse square law "is due to interference between the direct and reflected components of the wave field, the spatial variation is expected to occur on length scales comparable to a fraction of a wavelength" (p. 889). Measured average zero-crossing spacing of the deviation pattern = 29–41 % of a wavelength (Table XI: 40.8 % at 80 Hz, 29.2 % at 1–2 kHz, ~36 % at 6.3–10 kHz).
- Consequence: 0.3 m (1 ft) discrete sampling misses the structure; at 4 kHz the discrete traverse "would indicate no performance issues … while the continuous traverse identifies clear violations of the tolerance limits within 1.5 m of the source" (p. 889). Recommended spacing: ≤15 % of a wavelength (Nyquist on the ~30 % zero-crossing scale) → continuous traverse for tones.
- Broadband noise: "much less spatial structure"; with the exception of 80 and 125 Hz "very little deviation … regardless of the analysis method" (p. 890). Table IX: at 500 Hz LNE traverse, 37 % of tone samples exceed tolerance (fixed ref) vs 0 % for noise. Conclusion: broadband qualification is "a marginal indicator"; "of dubious value … in chambers where sources with pure tone components are to be tested" (p. 892).
- Fit method: fixed-reference L(r)=L(r_ref)−20log(r/r_ref) vs optimal-reference L(r)=20log[a/(r−r0)] with a, r0 from a least-squares fit (Koidan & Hruska). Optimal reference "significantly improve[s] the apparent performance … for pure tone qualifications", mostly at 80 and 125 Hz (Table IX: 80 Hz LNE tone 56 % → 13 % out of tolerance). Caveat: fitted source offset r0 exceeding 2× the largest source dimension (draft ISO 3745 limit) coincided with poorly performing traverses (Table X).
- Near field: minimum start distance r = max(0.5, 95/f) m from (kr)² > 3; starting the fit at 0.5 m at 80 Hz "shows a strong near-field impact" (Fig. 17).
- Method detail: repeatability of two successive 1 kHz traverses σ = 0.07 dB; source stability monitored by a fixed reference mic (σ ≤ 0.35 dB for noise, ≤0.1 dB for tones, Table VIII); narrow band-pass (1/20 of centre frequency) on tones; rms over ~integer periods with ~100 rms values per wavelength.
- Traverse toward the door/wedge-basket hinge showed *less* deviation than the corner traverse: "the structure about the wedge basket and the door on the west wall are not significant reflectors" (p. 889).

### Bonfiglio & Pompoli 2013, JASA 134(1) 285–291 — Bonfiglio-Pompoli_2013_JASA_numerical-low-frequency-anechoic-chambers
- Method chain: (1) FEM of 4 wedges in a virtual impedance tube → normal-incidence surface impedance; (2) plane-wave oblique reflection factor R_pw(θ)=(ζcosθ−1)/(ζcosθ+1); (3) spherical-wave reflection coefficient R_sw = R_pw + (1−R_pw)F(p_e) (Chien & Soroka 1975) — "at very low frequencies, the sound field emitted by the sound source is still spherical within the cavity"; (4) complex-image-source sum up to 20th order, convergence when successive orders differ by <0.05 dB and <0.5°; 7th order sufficed.
- Why not FEM of the room: a simplified FEM room with plane-wave impedance boundaries "cannot be used to predict the internal sound field" (ref. 12) because the field is spherical at low frequency.
- Validation: chamber A (hemi-anechoic, 800 m³, measured cut-off 50 Hz, 1.71 m polyester wedges + 0.9 m air gap): decay along a corner direction at 40 and 50 Hz "quite satisfactory"; surface pressure map at 50 Hz agrees except within ~1 m of the source where differences are 1.5–5 dB (near-field/finite source). Chamber B (fully anechoic, 650 m³, designed for 100 Hz): agreement at 80 and 100 Hz.
- Observation: "in both the examined cases the frequencies lower than the cut-off limit still guarantee a free field condition in a reduced volume of the chamber up to a distance of around 1 m from the boundaries" (p. 291).
- Wedge design: λ/4 rule "is not always respected"; for flow resistivity >10 kPa·s/m² a wedge >85 cm is needed for 100 Hz (Table II: 0.52 m at 3000 Pa·s/m² → 0.88 m at 40 000). Truncating the tip by 15 % (12 cm) still keeps α_n > 0.99.

### Schneider 2009, JSV 320 990–1003 — Schneider_2009_JSV_numerical-prediction-anechoic-chamber-quality
- Facility: LMA Marseille, 5.4 × 6.3 × 11.4 m tip-to-tip, 3720 melamine wedges (0.3×0.3×0.4 m base + 0.7 m taper) in an iron-wire frame with 0.25 m air gap. Measured 20–200 Hz with a bass-reflex box 1.6 m above floor tips, pure sine tones, 0.2 m traverse step, reference mic 0.25 m from the driver.
- Deviation defined vs a *numerical free-field model of the actual source* (not vs a fitted 1/r): L_p = 20log|p_exp/p_inc|.
- Problems observed: "significant deviations from free-field conditions occur in the 100–160 Hz frequency range"; the chamber "was found to be asymmetric in the 170–190 Hz frequency range" (p. 993–994) — cannot be reproduced by any uniform-admittance model.
- Modelling lesson: local admittance from an impedance tube "failed to predict the quality of the chambers at frequencies below ~150 Hz, regardless of the admittance applied"; mounting conditions (wire frame) change melamine's behaviour because its frame moves. Three linings with α = 0.997, 1.0, 0.997 give "significant differences in the size of the 1.5 dB region" (Fig. 8) → the absorption coefficient alone is not a suitable index at low frequency; complex admittance is required. Fix: a non-local (matrix) admittance built from a FEM of the wedge array, localised to adjacent wedges (nT = 3), BEM of the room with 138 280 unknowns, <1.5 h per frequency; correctly predicted the 110–160 Hz perturbations, >80 % of in-region points right, ~10 % false positives.
- Source placement note: the free-field admittance of a spherical wave depends on source position → "the source should be placed as far as possible from the walls".

### Jiang, Zhang & Huang 2016, JSV 381 139–155 — Jiang_2016_JSV_wedge-design-and-chamber-simulation
- Key claim: "the 99% sound energy absorption standard for individual wedges under normal incidence condition does not ensure the chamber to be anechoic … 99% energy absorption means 10% pressure reflection, which is still very large. In some area inside the anechoic chamber, the multi-reflected sound accumulates and violates the 1.5 dB deviation easily" (p. 152).
- Numbers (9 × 8 × 7 m chamber, source at centre, wedges designed for 100 Hz): at 100 Hz only 56.7 % (wedge) / 66.2 % (UGFW) of the centre-to-vertex line is within ±1.5 dB; centre-to-wall-centre line is fully within tolerance. Effective cut-off using Xu's 75 %-of-line criterion: 128 Hz (wedge) vs 120 Hz (UGFW) — i.e. 20–28 % above the design cut-off. Deviation "grows in the region close to the wedges".
- Modified CSI: wedge normal surface impedance varies strongly with incidence angle (Fig. 5) → replaces locally-reacting R_pw with FEM-derived oblique R_pw and z_ns; improvement vs full FEM of a 4.5 × 4 × 3.5 m chamber at 140 Hz is "in the critical region, where the 1.5 dB deviation is violated".
- Wedge mechanism at 100 Hz: absorption peaks at the wedge base; sound is "bounced back to the air space between the wedges" and trapped toward the base; at 300 Hz absorption spreads toward the tip. UGFW (uniform-then-gradient flat wall) needs 0.762 m vs 0.800 m depth for 100 Hz and less depth for 100–250 Hz cut-offs; realisable as 10 discrete layers.

### Wang & Tang 1996, Eng. Anal. Boundary Elements 18 103–110 — Wang-Tang_1996_EABE_BEM-sound-absorbing-wedges
- BEM of a single wedge in a tube with Delany–Bazley equivalent fluid; validated against Koidan et al. and AMSJ data; cut-off defined as |R| = 0.1.
- Findings: cut-off "strongly affected by the length of the wedge and the flow resistance"; base thickness and air-gap thickness (0–20 cm) have little effect on cut-off but raise overall absorption; hybrid (two-material) wedges lower the cut-off in the same depth; cutting 10–20 cm off the tip changes little, 30 cm pushes |R| to ~0.1 and "the wedge is not suitable".

### Vorländer 2013, JASA 133(3) 1203–1213 — Vorlander_2013_JASA_room-simulation-concepts-uncertainties
- Abstract: "prediction of reverberation times with accuracy better than the just noticeable difference requires input data in a quality which is not available from reverberation room measurements"; "it is not adequate to 'calibrate' a computer model by modification of input data" (p. ~1208).
