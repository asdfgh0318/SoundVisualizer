### Winker & Stahnke 2016, Inter-Noise 2016 — internoise2016_winker_iso3745_vs_iso26101.txt

- **Facility / scope**: Method/standards paper by ETS-Lindgren comparing chamber-qualification procedures in ISO 3745 Annex A (2012 revision) vs ISO 26101:2012. Two case-study chambers: (a) an operational hemi-anechoic chamber, internal 7.8 × 6.3 × 5.2 m (L×W×H), originally qualified 100 Hz–20 kHz to 2.0 m by ISO 3745:2012 (§4.1.1, p. 5); (b) a small anechoic chamber for audio/telephone testing, 3.35 × 3.05 × 2.74 m, specified ISO 3745 range 160 Hz–20 kHz to 1 m (§4.1.2, p. 5). Wedge depth not stated. Tolerances quoted from ISO 3745 Table 1 (p. 2): anechoic ±1.5 dB ≤630 Hz, ±1.0 dB 800–5000 Hz, ±1.5 dB ≥6300 Hz; hemi-anechoic ±2.5 / ±2.0 / ±3.0 dB.
- **Problems observed**:
  - ISO 3745 Annex A curve fit lets the "collinear offset of the acoustic centre" r0 float without a hard limit (the standard only says it "should" fall within 200 mm), so "a chamber can fully comply with ISO 3745 at certain frequencies while not exhibiting free-field performance at those frequencies" (§2.2, p. 3). The small chamber "analyzed to ISO 3745 at 160 Hz using the ability to calculate an abnormal acoustic offset" passes; analysed to ISO 26101 the deviation from inverse square law is "clearly seen before the half-wavelength distance of 1.1 m" (§4.1.2, p. 5).
  - ISO 26101's requirement to traverse to λ/2 of the lowest frequency limits the small chamber to 214 Hz instead of 200 Hz "due to geometry constraints", not acoustics (p. 5); for the large chamber the half-wavelength distance at 100 Hz is 1.7 m (p. 5).
  - High-frequency traverses (above 10 kHz, pure-tone qualification) become the limiting factor in large chambers: "The most likely factor is the influence of the measurement system inside the free-field creating reflections that do not appear at lower frequencies. A testing stand or rig is required to qualify most anechoic chambers..." (§4.2, pp. 7–8). "This situation occurs during pure tone qualifications and is not present in broadband qualifications due to signal averaging" (p. 7). "When using the ISO 26101 curve fit method, it is often impossible to fit the high frequency data" (p. 8).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: ISO 26101 fixes the slope to −20 lg(r/r0) with only the level parameter b free (Eq. 5–6, p. 3–4), removes acoustic-centre adjustment, requires ≥5 traverses plus extra paths for geometric abnormalities, contiguous one-third-octave analysis, spacing ≤ λ/10 below 1 kHz and 25 mm above 1 kHz, traverse start up to λ/4 from the acoustic centre (ISO 3745 starts at 0.5 m), mandatory source-stability correction from a fixed reference microphone (§3.1, pp. 3–4). Authors recommend dropping the λ/2 traverse-length requirement and widening the >10 kHz deviation window to match the ±5 dB directivity allowance above 10 kHz (Table 2, p. 8; §5).
- **Interpretation guidance**: "The range under which the chamber follows inverse square law and where it begins to deviate from inverse square law is obvious without employing either curve fit methodology" (p. 5) — read the raw level-vs-distance curve against a fixed −6 dB/doubling line; a fitted line with a movable r0 hides the departure. HF deviations that appear only with pure tones and vanish with broadband are attributed to fixture reflections averaged out over the band (p. 7).
- **Quotable sentences**: "By using this fitting method, a chamber can fully comply with ISO 3745 at certain frequencies while not exhibiting free-field performance at those frequencies." (p. 3). "Unless this change to the high-frequency requirements is implemented, chambers will be unnecessarily limited in performance by the method and apparatuses used instead of by the actual acoustic performance of the chamber." (p. 8).

### Haasjes (PhD thesis, Univ. of Twente, c. 2024; year not printed in text read) — utwente_haasjes_active_acoustic_anechoic_chamber.txt

- **Facility / scope**: Method thesis: extend the usable low-frequency range of an anechoic chamber (AAC) with feedforward active noise control, using the Kirchhoff–Helmholtz integral (KHI) on a ring of microphones to compute the reflected field. Reference facility: University of Twente anechoic chamber (Fig. 1.1, p. 2; dimensions not stated in sections read). Experimental rig: 2-D box 0.92 × 0.92 m, height 20 cm ("considered two-dimensional up to about 850 Hz"), glass lid 1.2 cm, 12 secondary Visaton W130X sources on the edges (3 per side, 0.3 m spacing), 12 primary sources fed through 1 m tubes to the centre, 12 sensors on a circle each with 3 radial microphones (§6.2, pp. 95–96). Numerical models: 0.84 m square room, walls Z = 10ρc, 12 channels, sensors on r = 0.24 m (§5.3, pp. 78–80); 5 × 5 m room, 200 channels, sensors at rs = 1.4 m (§5.4, pp. 90–91). Standard referenced: ISO 3745 (p. 2).
- **Problems observed**:
  - "a typical acoustic anechoic chamber has a lower cut-off frequency of up to 200 Hz, meaning that free-field conditions are not guaranteed below this frequency" (Summary, p. i). Wedge cut-off (10 % pressure reflection / 99 % absorption at normal incidence, measured in an impedance tube) is "not necessarily equal to the cut off frequency of the AAC" (§1.2, p. 3).
  - Literature cases cited: a chamber designed for 75 Hz cut-off "showed perturbations in the 110 to 160 Hz frequency range. Therefore, the cut off frequency lies at 200 Hz rather than 75 Hz. One of the identified causes of imperfections is the doors in the AAC, even though they are fitted with acoustic lining" (§1.2, pp. 3–4). Harvard chamber: deviations from inverse square law "below about 100 Hz"; UNAM chamber: largest deviations "below 200 Hz, and increase for decreasing frequencies", depending on source location and distance (p. 4).
  - Fresnel-volume argument: if the chamber volume is smaller than the Fresnel volume around the direct path, forward-scattered amplitudes are affected (p. 3).
  - Scattering-filter identification by removing the diffracting object (Friot et al.) is "ill-conditioned" and impossible for chamber walls (§1.4, p. 10).
  - Real-time rig: active control "increases the noise floor" after the impulse response has decayed (§6.3, p. 112); secondary-source spacing of 0.3 m limits accurate secondary-field generation to 570 Hz instead of 600 Hz (p. 102).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: KHI on a closed contour of pressure + particle-velocity sensors (velocity estimated from two microphones 0.01 m either side, Eq. 3.27): for a source inside the contour it yields the reflected field, for a source outside the total field (Summary; §1.8.1, p. 15). Spatial-Nyquist spacing dmax = c/(2 fmax) = 0.286 m for 600 Hz (p. 79). Three offline algorithms for the FIR control filters (FD-RMFE, PBC-CG, CC-CG; Ch. 4). Results: small-scale numerical 15.4 dB (13.8 dB without artificial delay) reduction of the KHI-computed reflected field (§5.3, p. 84); large-scale 200-channel numerical 13.4 dB average (§5.4, p. 92); real-time 9.6 dB (PBC-CG, §6.3, p. 112) and 9.4 dB (CC-CG, §6.4, p. 117) average at performance sensors up to ~600 Hz; T10-based RT reduced from 0.036/0.037/0.036 s to 0.022/0.016/0.020 s (PBC-CG, p. 113) and 0.037/0.036/0.036 s to 0.026/0.017/0.023 s (CC-CG, p. 118–119). Future: 3-D real-time, particle-velocity sensors to lower the limit further, effect of secondary sources on HF reflections unstudied (Ch. 7, pp. 122–123).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Free-field is "6 dB for every doubling of the distance"; inverse-square-law traverses are "the most common method to calibrate an AAC" (§1.1–1.2, pp. 1–3). Reverberation time (Schroeder integration, T10 fitted from −5 to −15 dB to "avoid the influence of direct sound or strong early reflections") is used as the indicator that reflections are suppressed; spectra at verification sensors "become increasingly flat" / "resonances have been suppressed" when control is on (§6.3–6.4, pp. 112, 117). A rise in RT and spectral ripple therefore indicate reflections; a flattening spectrum indicates their removal.
- **Quotable sentences**: "At this point it can be concluded that AACs suffer from low-frequency sound waves (up to about 200 Hz) reflecting off the walls, in some cases even above the cut off frequency the chamber is designed for." (§1.2, p. 4). "Existing AAC's suffer from low-frequency reflections of up to 200 Hz [18,31,32], due to limitations of the passive absorption material." (§1.6.1, p. 13).

### Merino-Martínez et al. 2020, Applied Acoustics 170:107504 — appacoust2020_merino-martinez_tudelft_anechoic_wind_tunnel.txt

- **Facility / scope**: TU Delft A-tunnel: open-jet, closed-circuit, vertical wind tunnel; anechoic plenum floor ≈ 6.4 × 6.4 m, height 3.2 m (§2, p. 2). Walls, ceiling and floor covered with Flamex foam wedges, total wedge height 0.49 m, designed on the λ/4 criterion for free-field "above approximately 173.5 Hz" (p. 4); floor foam under a 10 mm metal grid. Five interchangeable nozzles (0.25×0.4 m up to Ø0.6 m; Table 1, p. 4). 64-mic G.R.A.S. 40PH array on perforated steel plates (51 % open area), plate borders covered with foam. Free-field test per ISO 3745 tolerances (Table 5, p. 12). Conclusion quotes "cutoff frequency of 200 Hz" (§6, p. 14).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Inverse-square test (Visaton K50 speaker 0.5 m above nozzle, white noise, mic on guide wire 1→2 m in 0.1 m steps): bands ≥250 Hz meet ISO 3745 over the full range; 125, 160 and 200 Hz "only show acceptable values up to r = 1.5 m" (§5.2, p. 12, Fig. 16).
  - Standing wave of the jet column: HWA spectral peaks at 168.8 Hz (40×70 nozzle) and 125 Hz (Ø60 nozzle) that "do not change in frequency with the mean flow velocity V, and only their magnitude is increased ... attributed to the standing wave created by the jet column. In fact, the indicated frequency scales with the distance between the jet and the collector" (§4.3, p. 8).
  - Background noise: tone at 890 Hz at 10 m/s protruding ~8 dB above broadband, located by acoustic imaging "near the collector and the fan room"; smaller peak at 325 Hz at higher velocities; 63 Hz hump probably mains hum (§5.1, p. 9). Electronic-noise spectral peaks at 150, 250, ..., 4450 Hz and 16 kHz present even at 0 m/s (p. 8). A-weighting removes up to 30 dB, "indicating the strong low-frequency noise content" (p. 10). Velocity exponent k = 6.2–6.9 (Table 3), below the ideal 8, i.e. sources other than pure jet noise (p. 10).
  - Reverberation from the settling chamber through the open nozzle (T60 = 0.22 s measured with nozzle open; §5.3, p. 12).
  - General: closed sections give TBL pressure fluctuations "few orders of magnitude higher" than the acoustic signal plus reflections; open jets need refraction and spectral-broadening corrections through the shear layer (§1, p. 2).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Wedge height ≥ λ/4 of design cut-off (p. 4); foam-wrapped array frame and perforated plates to limit reflections (p. 5–6); splitter silencers (3 blocks 0.2 m wide, 2.5 m long, 2.6 m high, 0.1 m apart) and foam-lined collector (p. 4); settling-chamber lining "preventing any reflection ... between 150 Hz to 20 kHz" (p. 2). Array kept 1 ≤ h ≤ 1.5 m from the jet axis "to avoid contact with the shear layer" (p. 5). Recommendation: "For purely acoustic measurement not requiring flow, it is recommended to cover the nozzle to minimize the reverberation originating from the settling chamber" (p. 12). PSF check of the array at 2 and 4 kHz vs simulation to confirm no reflections ("no physical sound source is observed in the dynamic range selected (18 dB)", p. 13). SNR ≥ 10 dB desired (p. 9).
- **Interpretation guidance**: A spectral peak whose frequency is independent of flow speed but scales with the jet-to-collector distance is a jet-column standing wave, not a flow source (p. 8). Peaks present at 0 m/s are electronic. Low-frequency bands failing only beyond a certain radius indicate wall reflections becoming comparable to the direct field at larger r; measurements are "acceptable in that distance range" (p. 12).
- **Quotable sentences**: "The results for the three next one-third-octave bands below 250 Hz, (125 Hz, 160 Hz and 200 Hz) only show acceptable values up to r = 1.5 m." (§5.2, p. 12). "It is observed that the peaks do not change in frequency with the mean flow velocity V, and only their magnitude is increased. This is attributed to the standing wave created by the jet column." (§4.3, p. 8).

### Simmons, Jobling & Payne 2004, NPL Report DQL-AC 007 — npl_hemianechoic_sound_power_uncertainties.txt

- **Facility / scope**: NPL hemi-anechoic chamber (Annex A holds the specification; not in the text layer read). Room progressively degraded by removing wedges in 11 states (0, 40, 120, 224, 336, 560, 1008, 1300, 1450, 2000, 2626 = 100 %; Table 3, p. 21). Qualification per ISO 3745:1977 and ISO 3745:2003 inverse-square traverses (tolerances ±2.5 dB ≤630 Hz, ±2.0 dB 800–5000 Hz, ±3.0 dB ≥6300 Hz; Table 2, p. 15); sound power per ISO 3744 on a 1.5 m hemisphere with 10 key positions; K2 per ISO 3744. Sources: floor-flush "cavity point source" (2 mm aperture through the 0.5 m slab, tones 125 Hz–10 kHz at 50 dB, or flat broadband at 40 dB), B&K 4204 RSS, 550 W drill, RSS in an MDF box (§3.1, pp. 6–9).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Tones deviate more than broadband: "broad-band noise deviates less than pure tones, which is most likely due to averaging of the interference between the limits of the frequency band being analysed, whereas for tones such an effect is not allowed for" (§4.2.1, p. 22). Max pure-tone deviations (ISO 3745:2003) reach 9.3 dB at 250 Hz and 14.3 dB at 8 kHz with 1450 wedges removed (Table 5, p. 25); broadband max stays ≤3.7 dB (Table 4).
  - Decay slope drops from ~6 dB/doubling to ~3 dB/doubling when all wedges are removed; deviations largest at large r "where the ratio of direct to reflected signal is increasing with radius towards the room boundary" (p. 22).
  - Room fails tones above 250 Hz at ~20 % wedge removal (state 6); below 250 Hz before that (p. 27). Broadband (ISO 3745:1977) passes 250 Hz–4 kHz to ~50 % removal; "Surprisingly, at 8 kHz and 10 kHz, all room states were assessed as acceptable for testing broadband noise sources" (p. 27).
  - ISO 3745:2003 regression with free r0 passes "all room states ... at all frequencies except 125 Hz, even when all the absorptive wedges had been removed" — the fit "may not have a gradient corresponding to 6 dB per doubling of distance" (p. 28, Fig. 16). With the DIS limit r0 < half the source dimension (0.003 m), broadband "could not be achieved at any frequency even for room state 1" (p. 29).
  - Cavity source acts as a resonator (spectral corrections needed); RSS fails directivity below 1 kHz except 250 Hz; low-frequency directivity of the cavity source appears poor, attributed to the method and "reduced hemi-anechoic room performance at low frequencies" (p. 8).
  - K2A rises to 7.2 dB after ~50 % removal — from 0.4 dB per p. 32 (state 7 value in Table 8, p. 31) or from 1.3 dB per the conclusions, p. 51 (state 8 value); the report is inconsistent on the starting point; reverberation time only measurable after >50 % removal (p. 32). Sound power levels only rise significantly after ~half the wedges are gone (Tables 9–11, p. 35).
  - Uncertainty: A-weighted ΔLmax 0.8/1.5/1.3 dB (drill/box/RSS) for rooms qualifying under 1977, but 6.4/7.3/7.2 dB under 2003; standard uncertainty (rectangular, ΔLmax/√3) 0.5/0.8/0.8 dB vs 3.7/4.2/4.2 dB (p. 45). For rooms qualifying for tones: ~0.02 dB (p. 48). "the procedure for qualifying rooms using the broad-band noise methodology is over-tolerant of poor acoustic room performance" (p. 49).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Floor-flush cavity source "To eliminate interference effects it was necessary to locate the acoustic centre of the source, flush with the ground plane" (§3.1.1, p. 6); levels set from four heights on a 1.5 m hemisphere "averaged in order to reduce cancellation effects from the ground plane" (p. 8). Fixed overhead monitor microphone for source stability (§3.2.1, p. 11). Four array rotations by 30° (37 positions) for spatial averaging (§3.4.2.1, p. 17). Correcting with K2 from an RSS: A-weighted ΔLmax falls from 6.4/7.3 dB to 0.8/0.1 dB (ISO 3745:2003 rooms) (§4.4.2.4, p. 49); proposed K2 < 0.5 dB as a hemi-anechoic criterion (p. 50). Recommendation: remove Annex A decay-rate qualification from ISO 3745:2003 and develop a new method (§6, p. 53). Practical: removing a few wedges surrounded by wedges may not hurt performance — useful for cable/power provision (pp. 20, 51).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Compare traverse levels to a fixed −6 dB/doubling line (Eq. 8–9, p. 26), not to a free-slope regression; shallower slope means reflected energy; deviations grow with r; tones expose interference that one-third-octave averaging of broadband hides; standard deviation across array rotations (<0.5 dB, A-weighted <0.1 dB) does not reveal room influence — the absolute level offset ΔL between rooms does (§4.4.2, pp. 38–42).
- **Quotable sentences**: "It is shown that broad-band noise deviates less than pure tones, which is most likely due to averaging of the interference between the limits of the frequency band being analysed, whereas for tones such an effect is not allowed for." (p. 22). "Clearly, the current room qualification procedure as specified in ISO 3745:2003 is inadequate and significant errors in sound power level determination may occur." (p. 45). "The use of a RSS in a sound power level determination provides a much more reliable result, even when measurements are carried out in rooms that are clearly not hemi-anechoic." (p. 52).

### Jenny 2011, BYU senior thesis (advisor B.E. Anderson) — byu2011_jenny_ultrasonic_anechoic_chamber_qualification_thesis.txt

- **Facility / scope**: BYU small anechoic chamber, working dimensions 3.57 × 2.88 × 2.59 m, open-cell foam wedges (Future Foam 70200YW13, 0.032 g/cm³), base 30.48 cm, alternately oriented (§II, p. 4). Qualification 6.3 kHz–100 kHz one-third-octave bands per ANSI S12.55/ISO 3745 (which stops at 20 kHz); sources KEF Hypertweeter and Parasonics 40/58/75 kHz piezo transducers; G.R.A.S. 40BE ¼″ mic from 0.5 to 1.0 m in 5 cm steps along five corner traverses; swept sine below 50 kHz, random noise above (Table 1, p. 7).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Atmospheric absorption: "at ultrasonic frequencies it becomes clearer that atmospheric absorption is an issue. Shorter wavelengths and larger distances provide increasing divergence from theoretical predictions" (§III.A, p. 8); with the fixed-reference method (FRM) "The FRM only qualifies when absorption is accounted for" (p. 10).
  - Optimal-reference method (ORM, ISO 3745 fit with r0) gives non-physical acoustic-centre offsets: r0 = 13.7 cm (absorption accounted) and 20.6 cm (not) — "these estimates are clearly not accurate as the acoustic center cannot be located that far away from the physical surface of the transducer" (p. 10–11).
  - Source directivity: ka = 0.73 at 6.3 kHz (Hypertweeter) but 33.7 at 80 kHz (75 kHz transducer); ISO allows ±1.5/2.0/2.5/5.0 dB directivity deviation (630 Hz / 800–5000 / 6300–10000 / >10 kHz). Average deviation from omnidirectionality 22.5 dB (max 72.2 dB) without beam blocker, 10.6 dB (max 38.2 dB) with (§III.B, p. 12). A directional source "likely does not illuminate the entire chamber and thereby can be considered to provide a pseudo qualification" (p. 8).
  - Bands ≥63 kHz "commonly yield levels that exceed the allowable ±1.5 dB range with the beam blocker in place" (p. 12). "there appears to be evidence of interference from x = 70 cm and in the up and down swings in the data, though it is uncertain what degree of interference is present with the coarse spacing" — 9.2 wavelengths between points at 63 kHz (pp. 12–13).
  - Spacing: continuous traverse at 0.15 λ would need points every 1.03 mm at 50 kHz (p. 6).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Account for air absorption (Bass et al.) using measured temperature/humidity before band averaging (p. 6); prefer FRM at ultrasonic frequencies, possibly with a user-set offset (p. 11); aim the source at each traverse; washer "beam blocker" 3–4 cm in front of the transducer to spread the beam (p. 12); accept qualification with directional sources only if the chamber will be used with similarly directional sources "as long as this is understood when reporting data" (p. 13). Cites Cunefare/Badertscher and Wittstock/Bethke: pure tones matter because broadband averaging "suppresses interference, and therefore the deviation from the inverse square law" (§I, p. 2).
- **Interpretation guidance**: "up and down swings" along the traverse are read as interference, but with coarse spacing the true deviation may be larger; the ORM can absorb a reflection-induced slope change into a fake acoustic-centre shift — check that r0 is physically plausible (p. 11).
- **Quotable sentences**: "When atmospheric absorption is not accounted for, the optimal reference method employs nonphysical estimates of the acoustic center of the source in order to provide a spherical spreading fit to the data." (§IV, p. 15). "The high directivity of the source likely does not illuminate the entire chamber and thereby can be considered to provide a pseudo qualification for an anechoic chamber." (§III, p. 8).

### Friot & Gintz (CNRS-LMA), arXiv 2009 (conference-style paper; venue unclear in text) — arxiv2009_noise_reflections_global_control.txt

- **Facility / scope**: Method paper: estimating and actively cancelling scattered/reflected noise via "scattering filters" derived from the integral (Kirchhoff) representation of scattered pressure; experiments: duct with end tap (1-D), a parallelepiped in the LMA anechoic room (3-D, 14 channels), and a room mock-up (32 wall microphones, 100–300 Hz design). Target: LMA anechoic room 10 × 7 × 6 m with ~1400 wedges, free-field above 100 Hz; goal of active wall-reflection control in 20–100 Hz (§4, p. unclear; abstract).
- **Problems observed**:
  - "usual rooms do not allow anechoic measurements below 50Hz" (§4).
  - Time-windowing to separate reflections "failed, mainly because at low frequency echoes from a scattering body are very difficult to separate from actuator responses" (§2.B).
  - Removing the scattering body to measure the direct field is impossible for chamber walls (§2.B).
  - Dipole null-plane identification worked for rigid walls but "the inversion process proved to be to ill-conditioned in the case of absorbing walls" — with absorbing material the null-plane pressure was too low, "no filters could be identified" (§2.B, §4).
  - Global control fails at the resonance frequencies of the volume enclosed by the minimization microphones (§2.A); duct control ineffective below 100 Hz (insufficient actuator authority) and above 750 Hz (second duct mode) (§3.A). About 3 transducers per wavelength are needed → ~100 microphones and loudspeakers for 100 Hz in the 10×7×6 m room (§4).
  - Causality and the need for a feedforward reference "might be problematic for some primary sources such as aeroacoustic noise sources" (§5).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Estimate scattered pressure by linear filtering of ordinary microphone signals (Eq. 3); identify filters offline by regularized inversion; 3-D experiment achieved ~10 dB average reduction of scattered pressure at 280 Hz (§3.B, Fig. 4b). Proposed set-up (Fig. 7): detection microphones at the wedge surface, secondary loudspeakers in the walls; identify filters with the primary source off — "the linear filters that maps, when the primary source is off, the total acoustic pressure on S to the total pressure at a measurement microphone are the same as the linear filter that maps the pressure on S to the scattered pressure ... when the primary source is on" (§5). Microphone calibration not needed. Fallback: compute and subtract the scattered noise in post-processing rather than real-time (§5).
- **Interpretation guidance**: In the mock-up, "cavity resonances have been largely reduced in the estimated direct pressure, which indicates that the scattered field have been reasonably estimated" (§4, Fig. 6) — i.e. removing the reflected part removes the room-mode peaks from the spectrum.
- **Quotable sentences**: "Indeed, usual rooms do not allow anechoic measurements below 50Hz whereas such measurements would be desirable for many industrial noise sources or for validation of acoustic theoretical models." (§4). "methods involving windowing of time responses have failed, mainly because at low frequency echoes from a scattering body are very difficult to separate from actuator responses." (§2.B).

### Jawahar, Hanson, Akhter & Azarpeyvand 2025, Scientific Reports 15:2170 (file dated 2024; DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-82876-9) — scirep2024_porous_ground_propeller_ground_effect.txt

- **Facility / scope**: University of Bristol aeroacoustic facility anechoic chamber, 7.9 × 5.0 × 4.6 m including the acoustic walls (p. 3). APC 10×5.5″ pusher propeller (R = 127 mm), 7000 RPM (BPF = 233.33 Hz), axis 1.2 m above the chamber's perforated mesh platform. Ground plane: 1.7 × 1.5 m, 12 mm MDF plate at L/R = 0.75–4, bare or covered with 45PPI, 75PPI (12 mm) or 75PPI-T (24 mm) foam. Far-field polar array of 23 G.R.A.S. 40PL mics at 1.75 m (≈13R), θ = 40° above to 150° below the plate; 8 flush surface mics S1–S8 at 50 mm spacing in the plate. PSD with Hanning window, Δf = 2 Hz, 16 s at 2^16 Hz (pp. 3–4).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Ground-plane reflection: even out of ground effect (L/R = 4) "the GE configuration exhibits a significant increase in TSSPL by approximately 4.0–8.0 dB for frequencies above 1000 Hz" at θ = 50°, "likely due to the reflection of noise from the ground plane, which is observed in both solid and porous configurations. As expected, this increase is absent at θ = 90°, where the sideline region does not experience reflections" (p. 9). OASPL: "The solid ground plate amplifies OASPL by ≈3 dBA at shallow angles" at L/R = 1 (p. 6). Two zones: reflection zone above the plate, shielded zone below (θ > 100° "sharply decreases ... as the microphones are shielded by the ground plane", p. 6).
  - Aerodynamic ground effect: CT and CP rise exponentially for L/R ≤ 2; porous surfaces add up to 30 %/15 % over solid (p. 5). Extra mid-frequency harmonics (600–2500 Hz, m = 6–9) appear with the plate, attributed to turbulence ingestion/recirculation and BVI; broadband humps 3.8–13 kHz; a peak >10 kHz is motor mechanical noise (pp. 7–8). Coherence between surface mics drops in IGE (chaotic flow) (p. 11).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Porous treatments: 75PPI-T reduces far-field sideline TSSPL by ~13 dB in 1.5–10 kHz at L/R = 0.75, and OASPL increment by ≈2 dBA at shallow angles; near-field reductions up to 26–36 dB low-frequency and 6–10 dB mid/high (pp. 9–11). Thrust-scaled SPL (TSSPL, Eq. 2) used to compare configurations at different thrust. Propeller mounted 1.2 m above the mesh floor "to prevent interference with the propeller's airflow" (p. 3).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Distinguish reflection from aerodynamic ground effect by angle and distance: a level increase present at shallow angles and absent at 90°, and present already at L/R = 4 where thrust is unchanged, is acoustic reflection; changes that scale with L/R ≤ 2 and with thrust (stronger BPF tone, new harmonics, broadband humps) are flow interaction (pp. 6, 9). Broadband humps that appear only with the plate are wake/shear-layer interaction, not reflections.
- **Quotable sentences**: "At θ = 50°, the GE configuration exhibits a significant increase in TSSPL by approximately 4.0–8.0 dB for frequencies above 1000 Hz. This increase is likely due to the reflection of noise from the ground plane, which is observed in both solid and porous configurations." (p. 9). "The solid ground plate amplifies OASPL by ≈3 dBA at shallow angles, while porous treatments reduce this increment by ≈2 dBA" (p. 6).

### Belyaev, Golubev, Zverev, Makashov, Palchikovskiy, Sobolev & Chernykh 2015, Akusticheskii Zhurnal 61(5):636–644 (Acoustical Physics 61(5):606–614) — acoustphys2015_belyaev_wedge_absorption_anechoic_ru.txt (read in Russian; OCR text)

- **Facility / scope**: Diffuse-field absorption of anechoic-chamber wedges measured per ISO 354:2006 in the TsAGI reverberation chambers AK-3 (V = 125 m³) and AK-11 (V = 210 m³), for lining the PNIPU (Perm) anechoic chamber with flow, 11.8 × 8.2 × 5.3 m (p. 637). Baseline: wedges of the operating TsAGI AK-2 anechoic facility — wire frame, capron fibre 80–90 kg/m³, total height 600 mm on a 200 × 200 × 150 mm base, hung 100 mm from the wall; αn > 0.99 for f > 100 Hz except 160 Hz (αn = 0.978); AK-2 is certified per ISO 3745:2003 "for the frequency range ≥ 200 Hz" and "provides free-field conditions for frequencies above 200 Hz" (pp. 637–638). Candidate wedges: basalt super-thin fibre (BSTV, fibre ≤ 1–2 µm, 20/30/40 kg/m³) 800 mm high, 200 mm wide, 1000 mm long, 100 mm base; mineral thin fibre (MTV, 5–9 µm fibre, 30/50/70 kg/m³), same geometry, plus a version 30 cm taller (110 cm) (pp. 639–640).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Wedge height must exceed λ/4 at the planned lower limit; other parameters (material, base width, air gap) need separate study; Russia had "lost the competence of commercial production of wedges for large anechoic chambers" (p. 636–637).
  - No reliable method to convert normal-incidence to diffuse-field absorption (p. 637); different reverberation chambers give significantly different results, so AK-11 results were not directly compared with AK-3 results (p. 640).
  - 40 kg/m³ BSTV tests were "recognised as non-conforming" and dropped (p. 639–640).
  - The dimensionless coefficient α = A/S (A divided by wedge-block surface area 7.0 / 8.9 / 12.4 m²) stays < 0.7 over the whole range and only agrees between wedge heights above 1250 Hz, because the coefficient "has physical meaning only for a flat sample ... and not for a discrete absorber" (p. 642).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Use equivalent absorption area A(f) from ISO 354 (Eq. 1) and its 100–5000 Hz average Aavg (Eq. 2); test blocks in two orientations (rotated 45°) to average edge effects, five repeats per band (p. 638). Results: Aavg (AK-3) 2.9 m² for AK-2 wedges, 4.6 and 4.5 m² for BSTV 20 and 30 kg/m³ (Table 1, p. 642); Aavg (AK-11) 4.2 m² BSTV 20 kg/m³, 3.5/3.7/3.7 m² MTV 30/50/70 kg/m³, 4.4 m² for 110 cm MTV (Table 2). Glass-cloth cover (100 g/m²) slightly increases absorption and protects fragile mineral-fibre tips against flow; metal frame rod thickness 2 vs 4 mm made no difference (p. 640). Taller wedges absorb more (p. 641). BSTV wedges recommended and installed in the PNIPU chamber (Fig. 11, p. 643); expected free-field "not lower than 200 Hz" (p. 640).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Final quality is judged by ISO 3745:2003 certification, "determined by the deviation from the 1/R law" (p. 637); wedges with lower normal-incidence absorption generally have lower diffuse-field absorption (p. 637).
- **Quotable sentences** (translated): "The final conclusions on wedge quality are given by the certification results of the room lined with them, carried out according to ISO 3745:2003. The result is assessed by the deviation from the 1/R law" (p. 637). "The wedges of the AK-2 anechoic chamber ... provide free-field conditions for frequencies above 200 Hz" (p. 640).

### Bahr, Hutcheson & Stead 2020 (NASA NTRS; journal version of AIAA 2018-3118; journal name unclear in text) — ntrs2020_unsteady_propagation_openjet_kevlar.txt

- **Facility / scope**: NASA Langley Quiet Flow Facility, anechoic open-jet tunnel, 2 × 3 ft nozzle; open-jet vs tensioned Kevlar-49 Style 120 panel (34 fibres/inch, 0.003″, 1500 N/m) bounding one side of the flow. Laser-plasma pulsed point source (Nd:YAG 120 mJ) in the flow; arc of 1/8″ mics outside the flow at −45° … −135°; Mach 0, 0.13, 0.17; 100–101 pulses per case at 5 Hz; 150 Hz–100 kHz analog band, 250 kS/s (§III, pp. 3–5).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Shear-layer refraction (mean, deterministic) and turbulent scattering (stochastic): scattering "can appear as coherence loss ... For tonal acoustic signals, scattering broadens the otherwise sharp spectral shape ... In microphone phased array processing, this degradation can lead to a blurring effect in the source maps" (§II, p. 2–3). In time domain: pulse "spread" (shape change) and "wander" (arrival-time change).
  - Open-jet wander "significantly surpasses that of the Kevlar panel for all Mach numbers and angles", minimum at upstream angles, increasing downstream (§IV.A, p. 10). Open-jet spread CV reaches unity at high frequency for M = 0.17 and ≥10 at −60°/−45°, i.e. "strong scattering"; Kevlar CV ≤ 0.5 (weak scattering) (§IV.B, p. 11). Coherence drops to γ² ≈ 0.2 at 10 kHz for open jet at −45°, M 0.17, vs ≈ 0.7 with Kevlar (p. 12, 17). At 20 kHz the open-jet normalized cross-spectral term C is "effectively zero mean" (p. 17).
  - Kevlar: ringing after the pulse (deterministic panel response) and Doppler misalignment add ripple to the magnitude-correction curves (§IV.D, p. 18); background noise: similar to open jet at 3 kHz, ~5 dB higher at 10 kHz, >25 dB higher at 40 kHz (M 0.17) — "could completely mask a 40 kHz source" (§IV.E, p. 21; §V, p. 23).
  - Microphone grid cap distorts the pulse waveform significantly (p. 5).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Amiet's refraction method for propagation time/path (p. 10, 8); time-gating each pulse with a 600 µs 25 % Tukey window "to remove any reflected or scattered signals from the data, reducing analysis to a single propagation path" (§III.C.2, p. 7); shift pulses by arrival time to separate wander from spread; relative Kevlar/open-jet transmission coefficient T_rel using a 90° reference mic (Eq. 10) with atmospheric attenuation via ANSI S1.26 along the Galilean-transformed path (p. 8); magnitude-and-phase averaging or in-situ calibration reference (Sijtsma; Koop) for coherence loss (p. 17–18); for coherence-critical (phased array, distributed coherent sources) tests prefer Kevlar, for very quiet sources prefer open jet (§V, p. 22–23).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Plot block-by-block normalized cross-spectral terms C on the unit circle: points on the circle with scattered phase = wander only (weak scattering, correctable); magnitude scatter toward zero = spread/strong scattering (Fig. 8, p. 17). Downstream angles and higher Mach/frequency are worst. Doppler: positive shift and level increase downstream, negative upstream (p. 18–21).
- **Quotable sentences**: "The open-jet data show far more coherence reduction for a given angle and Mach number, dropping as low as γ² = 0.2 at 10 kHz for the −45° microphone at Mach 0.17." (§IV.C, p. 12). "The background noise production of the Kevlar is sufficiently high such that it could completely mask a 40 kHz source that is perfectly observable otherwise." (§V, p. 23).

### Bahr 2021 (NASA NTRS; AIAA conference paper, venue unclear in text) — ntrs2021_open_closed_test_section_array.txt

- **Facility / scope**: NASA Langley 14- by 22-Foot Subsonic Tunnel, CRM-HL high-lift model tested in closed-wall (2018; 110-mic array, 1.83 m aperture, 3.33 m from model, screen cover over the array) and open-jet (2020/21; 97-mic array, 2.00 m, 5.17 m from model) configurations at M 0.16 and 0.2; DAMAS deconvolution; 2–40 kHz, 96 Hz bins (§IV–V, pp. 5–7).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Closed section: "tunnel walls are approximately rigid in the acoustic sense, leading to significant reflections in the reverberant environment" (§I, p. 1); DAMAS maps show "image speckle"; solution energy on the top/bottom grid boundaries suggests "more than the initial floor and ceiling reflections may be significant, or that there are other unmodeled noise sources"; energy on the downstream boundary indicates tunnel background noise coherent across the array — "CSM diagonal treatments will not eliminate this" (§VI.A, p. 11). Cross-beamforming shows the slat tone's floor image is coherent but the ceiling image is not; broadband images appear incoherent (Fig. 4, p. 8–9). Screen cover may need "adjustments on the order of one or two dB as a function of frequency and angle of incidence" from transmission loss and "a minor standing wave pattern between the cover and microphone plate" (p. 5); no empty-tunnel background data acquired.
  - Open jet: "severe blurring due to the decorrelation of acoustic waves passing through a turbulent free shear layer" (abstract); at 10.6 kHz baseline DAMAS "begin[s] to fall apart due to coherence loss"; at 30 kHz conventional beamforming "completely fails to localize" and the open-jet DAMAS map "is, for the most part, unusable" (p. 11). Coherence loss underestimates summed levels: correction raises wing spectra ~2 dB and slat ~5 dB at high frequency (p. 12). Corrected closed vs open-jet slat spectra still differ by up to 8 dB at 40 kHz (p. 12).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Image-source Green's function (Eq. 14) or extended grid mirrored across floor/ceiling (24,030 points) for incoherent images (Eq. 16–17); mutual coherence function Γ from Wilson's model with von Kármán correlation, fitted to in-wing speaker coherence data (Eq. 21–23, Fig. 5), inserted into the DAMAS coefficient matrix (Eq. 20). Amiet shear-layer refraction in steering vectors for open jet; convective Helmholtz Green's function for closed. Hald diagonal optimization (closed) and subspace background subtraction (open jet). Recommendations: always acquire empty-tunnel background; quantify instrumentation covers; include known-source test points to build a coherence-loss model (§VII, p. 13). Relative (Mach-scaling) comparisons are insensitive to facility: ~5 dB at low frequency in all cases (≈ fifth-power) (§VI.C, p. 12).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Speckle in deconvolution maps and energy piling up on grid boundaries indicate unmodeled reflections or external noise; coherence of an image with the true source can be checked by cross-beamforming (tones tend to be coherent images, broadband incoherent); open-jet blurring grows with frequency; relative level changes are trustworthy where absolute levels are not.
- **Quotable sentences**: "The closed test section results show contamination likely due to background noise and reflections. The open-jet results show severe blurring due to the decorrelation of acoustic waves passing through a turbulent free shear layer." (abstract, p. 1). "Counterintuitively, improving the physics modeling in the propagation functions is detrimental to the agreement between the two facility configurations." (§VII, p. 13).

### Schlinker & Amiet 1978, NASA CR-145359 (UTRC) — ntrs1978_schlinker_amiet_shear_layer_refraction_experimental.txt (summary, introduction and conclusions only)

- **Facility / scope**: UTRC Acoustic Research Tunnel, 0.91 m diameter open jet, free-stream Mach 0.1–0.4; discrete-tone source 1–10 kHz (text layer reads "1 kHz to 10 Hz", OCR) placed in the airstream at several axial and two off-axis positions; wavefront angle measured by cross-correlating far-field microphones and ray-tracing back to an assumed zero-thickness cylindrical shear layer of nozzle radius; dipole source for amplitude correction; scattering test at 90° (Summary, pp. 1–2; Method of Approach, p. 5).
- **Problems observed**:
  - "The shear layer serves to refract, reflect, and scatter the sound radiated from the model. These effects significantly alter the acoustic source directivity pattern and hence alter the conclusions drawn from a particular experiment" (Introduction, p. 3). Refraction "significant at Mach numbers of 0.1 and greater" (p. 1; Conclusion 1, p. 37).
  - Off-axis source closer than one jet radius to the shear layer: measurable theory/experiment differences, attributed to sensitivity to the assumed shear-layer position (Conclusion 6).
  - Amplitude-correction theory "could not be verified due to limitations of the experimental technique" (Conclusion 7).
  - Turbulence scattering: "Significant discrete tone spectrum broadening and tone amplitude changes were observed at open jet Mach numbers above 0.2 and at acoustic source frequencies greater than 5 kHz. More severe turbulence scattering was observed for downstream source locations. Consequently, sound radiated at angles close to the jet axis will encounter greater turbulence scattering than that radiated at 90° to the open jet" (p. 2; Conclusion 8).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Amiet zero-thickness shear-layer angle correction validated for on-axis sources over M 0.1–0.4 (Conclusion 2); extended to off-axis sources (Appendix A); shear-layer thickness/divergence and frequency (1–10 kHz) need not be included (Conclusions 3–4; source positions 0.3–2 diameters downstream). Planar assumption valid "as long as the wavelength is smaller than the open jet radius" (Appendix A, p. 38). Below M 0.1 "measurements outside the airstream can be used to directly infer the source noise characteristics" (p. 3).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Tone broadening and amplitude change at a fixed observer grow with Mach number (>0.2), frequency (>5 kHz) and downstream source position; angle change is frequency-independent, so a frequency-dependent angular shift is not refraction.
- **Quotable sentences**: "Far-field noise directivity patterns measured in open-jet acoustic test facilities are significantly altered at test Mach numbers of 0.1 and greater due to sound wave refraction by the open-jet shear layer." (Conclusion 1, p. 37). "Scattering of sound by shear layer turbulence is significant at Mach numbers greater than 0.2 and frequencies greater than 5 kHz. Scattering effects increase as the source position is moved downstream." (Conclusion 8, p. 37).

### Moritani / HBK × Sonora technical column, 27 May 2025 (web) — web_acoustic-measurement_lower_limit_frequency.txt

- **Facility / scope**: Vendor explainer defining "lower limit frequency" vs "cutoff frequency" of an anechoic chamber; references ISO 3745:2003 Annex K3 vs ISO 3745:2012. No facility or data.

Cutoff frequency "is defined by the acoustic performance of the absorber alone: specifically, a normal-incidence absorption coefficient ≥ 0.99 and an absorber thickness of at least λ/4"; lower limit frequency "is determined by the overall acoustic absorption performance of the chamber, not just the absorber unit". - **Interpretation guidance**: None beyond the definitional distinction.
- **Quotable sentences**: "The cutoff frequency is defined by the acoustic performance of the absorber alone ... In contrast, the lower limit frequency is determined by the overall acoustic absorption performance of the chamber, not just the absorber unit."

### Singh, Garg & Narayanan 2020, Int. J. Aeroacoustics 19(1–2) — ijaeroacoustics2020_singh_lower_cutoff_frequency_empirical.abstract.txt — **abstract only**
Empirical expression for an anechoic chamber's lower cut-off frequency from source volume, chamber volume, wedge height and source-to-wall distance, agreeing within ±3 % with experiment; the chamber is qualified by inverse-square-law traverses in several directions and its cut-off is found to be 315 Hz within ±0.5 dB.
Fan broadband-noise spectra inside vs outside the chamber demonstrate its echo-free behaviour over a broad frequency range.

### Hanson, Jawahar, Vemuri & Azarpeyvand 2023, J. Sound Vib. 559:117751 — jsv2023_hanson_propeller_noise_ground_effect.abstract.txt — **abstract only**
Two-bladed propeller in ground effect in an anechoic chamber: thrust/torque/power rise strongly; far-field spectra show additional tonal peaks and, at some polar angles, high-frequency broadband humps not seen isolated; two regions — a shielded zone (OASPL reduced) and a reflection zone (OASPL enhanced), the latter "a result of pure acoustic reflection from the ground surface".
Ground-plane embedded microphones locate the dominant source in the tip region; near- and far-field coherence studied vs ground distance.

### Garg et al. 2019, MAPAN 34(3):357–369 — mapan2019_garg_microphone_freefield_calibration_uncertainty.abstract.txt — **abstract only**
A transportable 2 m³ wedge-lined SPEKTRA anechoic chamber is used for IEC 61094-8 substitution-method free-field microphone calibration over 125 Hz–20 kHz; the usable working space is identified with the inverse-square pressure law and deviations from it are quantified.
Expanded uncertainty ±0.36–0.52 dB (k = 2) validated in a bilateral comparison; Monte Carlo uncertainty evaluation demonstrated.

### Stroud 2010, AES Convention 129, Paper 8170 — aes2010_stroud_quasi_anechoic_notch_equalization.abstract.txt — **abstract only**
Gated (quasi-anechoic) loudspeaker measurement in ordinary rooms is limited because the driver's second-order high-pass response makes the impulse long, so reflections must be suppressed out to returns of 30 ms or more.
A tuned notch (band-cut) equalization shortens the impulse response and allows correct full-range measurement in moderately sized non-anechoic rooms.

### Ma, Chen, Wang, Liu & Ding 2022, Applied Acoustics 186:108424 — appacoust2022_ma_active_control_large_mppa_point_source.abstract.txt — **abstract only** (note: the DOI ...108383 supplied earlier resolves to a different paper)
Theory and experiment on actively improving low-frequency absorption of a large micro-perforated panel absorber by a point source in its cavity; absorption improves significantly "in a wide low frequency range below the cutoff frequency", with the optimal source position at the cavity-section centre (cutoff set by the short side length).
Mechanism: suppressing the cavity field enhances Helmholtz-type resonant absorption; higher-order cavity modes (except (0,0,0)) contribute nothing due to mode-shape symmetry; pressure-release and impedance-matching strategies remain applicable.

### UCMAR 2019, Acta Acustica united with Acustica (DOI 10.3813/AAA.919353) — actaacustica2019_ucmar_shear_layer_refraction.abstract.txt — **abstract only**
Shear-layer refraction in open-jet tunnels shifts the apparent source position in beamforming; a unified 3-D correction method (UCMAR) covers several traditional 1-D/planar forms and handles temperature gradients across the shear layer and off-axis sources in circular jets.
Validated by ray tracing and a benchmark; a time-reversed "reverse UCMAR" yields a quick acceptable solution.

### Singh, Garg & Narayanan 2020, Int. J. Aeroacoustics 19(1–2):57–72 — ijaeroacoustics2020_singh_lower_cutoff_frequency_empirical.txt

- **Facility / scope**: In-house plywood cuboid anechoic chamber at IIT (ISM) Dhanbad; external 2.75 × 2.40 × 2.98 m, working space 2.6 × 1.7 × 2.20 m tip-to-tip (p. 61). Lining: ~300 polyurethane-foam wedge blocks (32 FR foam, 30 ± 1 kg/m³), block 600 × 600 × 300 mm, wedge 275 mm on a 25 mm base, glued to walls and ceiling; floor is carpet with PU wedges laid on top to make it fully anechoic (Table 1, p. 61). Stated cut-off: 315 Hz "within 0.5 dB" by inverse-square-law verification along radial paths per ISO 3745:2012 (pp. 58, 66). Instrumentation: GRAS 40PH ¼″ free-field mic (50 mV/Pa), NI cDAQ 9174 + NI 9222, 10 s at 50 kHz, 1024-point Hanning FFT blocks averaged (p. 61). Source "with size smaller than 0.005 times the chamber size placed roughly at the center", described as generating "unidirectional sound" (p. 62; presumably omnidirectional — unclear in text). Signals: 500 Hz and 10 kHz sines with added white noise (Tables 2–3 quote "signal-to-noise ratio – 0.05"), mics at 20, 40, 80, 160 cm along paths OA and OB into two corners (pp. 62–63).
- **Empirical formula, exactly as printed** (OCR renders λ as "k"):
  - Blanco & Herráez semi-anechoic (their Eq. 2): `a + 2a + λ/4 + lw = d`; fully anechoic (Eq. 3): `a + 4a + λ/2 + 2lw = d` (p. 59).
  - Proposed (Eq. 4): `a + 2λ + λ/4 + lw = d` — the near-field term 2a is replaced by 2λ, "the approximate distance from the source at which far-field zone begins" (p. 59, citing a Siemens community web page, ref. 9).
  - Rewritten (Eq. 5): `f_chamber = 2.25c / (d − lw − a)`; and for V_chamber ≫ V_source (Eq. 6): `f_chamber = 2.25c / (d − lw)` (pp. 59–60).
  - Variables (Appendix 1, p. 72): a = source diameter (m); d = "major dimension (i.e., total distance between the source and the chamber wall, in m)"; lw = length of the wedge (m); λ = cut-off wavelength (m); c = speed of sound; V_source, V_chamber = volumes (m³).
  - Fitted / validated on: **one chamber only, their own** (d = 2.77 m, lw = 0.3 m per the Fig. 9 caption, p. 69). Table 4 (p. 68): Eq. 1 (c/4L) → 285 Hz (10.52 % deviation); Eq. 3 (Blanco) → 92 Hz (242 %); Eq. 4 → 320 Hz (1.60 %); Eq. 5 → 312 Hz (0.95 %); measured 315 Hz. Eq. 4 and Eq. 5 are algebraically identical, so why they give 320 vs 312 Hz is unclear in text (2.25 × 343 / (2.77 − 0.3) = 312 Hz, i.e. the 312 figure is the a-neglected Eq. 6 form). Note d = 2.77 m exceeds the 2.6 m tip-to-tip working length — d is measured to the chamber wall, not the wedge tips.
  - **±3 % claim, exact wording**: Abstract — "The frequency obtained from the empirical expression compares well within 3% of the lower cut-off frequency obtained from the experiments." (p. 57). Conclusions — "The frequency obtained from the current predictions matches very well with the actual cut-off frequency of the constructed anechoic chamber and is within 3%." (p. 70). No "±" appears in the paper; the earlier abstract-only entry's "±3 %" was an editorial addition.
- **Problems observed**:
  - Below cut-off the inverse-square slope collapses: Table 2 (500 Hz signal + noise) gives per-doubling slopes of 0.29 / 0.12 dB at 100 Hz and 3.02 / 3.65 dB at 200 Hz (corners 1 / 2), vs 5.67 / 6.17 dB at 315 Hz (p. 67); Table 3 (10 kHz signal) is nearly identical (0.01 / 0.30 dB at 100 Hz, 2.98 / 3.63 dB at 200 Hz, 5.44 / 6.23 dB at 315 Hz) (p. 68). The paper does not name the mechanism (reverberant/standing-wave build-up) — it only declares 315 Hz the cut-off.
  - Above cut-off the slopes scatter around 6 dB: 5.23–6.35 dB at 630–1000 Hz, 6.74–6.77 dB at 5 kHz, 5.34–5.86 dB at 10 kHz, and 7.30–7.62 dB at 15 kHz (Tables 2–3) — deviations of up to ~1.5 dB per doubling that the paper does not discuss; the >6 dB slopes at 15 kHz are consistent with air absorption but this is not stated in the text.
  - Background noise: "The chamber calibration must be performed in a quiet environment ... the presence of background noise will provide an extraneous value to the acoustic signals" (p. 62); background spectra shown to be "well above" in Figs. 4–5 (p. 65).
  - Small residual surfaces: "left-out edges and corners of the chamber are also properly covered with pieces of PU in order to avoid spurious reflections from the small surfaces" (p. 60).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Use Eq. 4/5 at design time instead of c/(4L) (Eq. 1 under-predicts by 10.5 % for this chamber; Blanco's Eq. 3 "underpredicts" grossly, p. 69). Chamber walls, ceiling and floor "must be air locked by the modules of the PU wedges" (p. 62). Verify by 1/3-octave SPL vs log2(r/a) linear fits (Eq. 19, slope m, correlation rc ≥ 0.97) rather than by single-frequency spectra (pp. 65–66). Fan broadband spectra inside vs outside show "reduced sound levels ... from mid- to high-frequency range (i.e., about 500 Hz to 20 kHz)" as a qualitative anechoicity check (p. 70).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Cut-off is read as the lowest 1/3-octave band whose fitted per-doubling slope is 6 dB within the tolerance (here 0.5 dB); a slope near 0 dB (100 Hz) means the field is not decaying with distance at all, a slope of ~3 dB (200 Hz) is transitional. Directional dependence is checked by repeating along two corner paths (OA, OB).
- **Quotable sentences**: "The lower cut-off frequency is defined as the minimum frequency from which the chamber shows its anechoic behavior." (p. 58). "The SPL decays by about 6 dB along the paths OA and OB, within about 0.5 dB at a low frequency of 315 Hz and is evident from the slopes shown in Tables 2 and 3." (p. 65). "Thus, equation (4) could be considered as the best alternate for the accurate prediction of the lower cut-off frequency of the anechoic chamber over equation (1)." (p. 70).

### Garg, Surendran, Dhanya, Chandran, Asif & Singh 2019, MAPAN 34(3):357–369 — mapan2019_garg_microphone_freefield_calibration_uncertainty.txt

- **Facility / scope**: SPEKTRA (Dresden) transportable anechoic chamber at FCRI Palakkad, 2 m³ internal, outside 2 × 2 × 2.4 m, aluminium/PU sandwich panels, fully lined with 35 cm polyurethane-foam wedges; theoretical lower cut-off 250 Hz "derived in theory from its dimensions and the characteristics of the materials" (pp. 358–359). Loudspeaker in one removable side panel, microphone through a hole in the opposite (absorber-lined) panel; two USB cameras to confirm mic position (p. 359). IEC 61094-8 substitution calibration 125 Hz–20 kHz; free-field verification per ISO 26101:2012 with a B&K 4191 mic + Microtech Gefell MV 203 preamp (mic diameter garbled, p. 360); 23 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10 % RH (p. 359).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Deviation from inverse-square law is strongly distance- and frequency-dependent at low frequency: at 125 Hz the deviation runs from +4.52 dB at 0.64 m through 0.06 dB at 0.84 m to −7.13 dB at 1.14 m; at 160 Hz +2.03 → −2.57 dB; at 250 Hz +0.97 → −2.47 dB (Table 2, p. 362). ISO 26101 tolerances (Table 1, p. 360: ±1.5 dB ≤ 630 Hz, ±1.0 dB 800–5000 Hz, ±1.5 dB ≥ 6300 Hz) are met **only between 0.79 and 0.89 m** in the low band; max deviations 1.1 dB at 0.89 m/125 Hz, 0.5 dB at 0.89 m/800 Hz, 0.3 dB at 0.79 m/12.5 kHz (p. 360). The entire 1.09 m column of Table 2 sits ~−0.8 to −1.3 dB below its neighbours at every band 315 Hz–20 kHz (Table 2) — not commented on in the text (unclear in text; looks like a positioning artefact).
  - "In the frequency range of 125–250 Hz, the acoustical properties of the room deteriorate gradually. The sound field in the chambers is still dominated by direct sound from the loudspeaker although in addition to that some reflections from the walls may occur due to reduction in sound absorption coefficient of the wall lining at lower frequencies" (p. 364). Radial checks: 1 cm sphere at 0.84 m — max 0.5 dB at 12.5 kHz (top), 0.4 dB at 125 Hz (0.83 m), ≤ 0.2 dB between 250 and 8000 Hz; 1 cm × 5 cm cylinder at 86.5 cm — max 0.63 dB (right, 125 Hz), 0.58 dB centre at 125 Hz, 0.30 dB at 1 kHz, < 0.30 dB elsewhere (pp. 360–361).
  - Position sensitivity: 0.035 dB/mm at 125 Hz, 0.024 dB/mm at 160 Hz, 0.02 dB/mm from 200 Hz–20 kHz; with ±1 mm placement/acoustic-centre error the contribution is 0.05 / 0.03 / 0.026 dB (p. 363).
  - Fixture/apparatus reflection and source/mic directionality appear in the cause-and-effect diagram (Fig. 8, p. 364) but are not quantified; "difference in the directivity pattern of both the reference and DUC is small, and thus, the contribution is neglected" (p. 364). Air attenuation contributes only 0.0004–0.0011 dB (Table 4, p. 366).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Define a working point at the axial centre (0.84 m from the loudspeaker front) where deviation is minimal (0.00–0.10 dB across all bands, Table 2) and restrict the working space to 0.79–0.89 m (p. 360). Carry the worst in-space deviation of 0.10–0.20 dB as a "departure from free-field characteristics" uncertainty term (0.115 dB standard, rectangular), the largest non-reference contributor in the budget; expanded uncertainty (k = 2) 0.45 dB at 125–200 Hz, 0.30 dB at 250 Hz–1 kHz, 0.36 dB at 1.25–5 kHz, 0.42 dB at 6.3–8 kHz, 0.48 dB at 10 kHz, 0.52 dB at 12.5–20 kHz (Table 4, p. 366; abstract quotes 0.36–0.52 dB). Validation: < 0.16 dB (B&K 4180) and < 0.25 dB (B&K 4191) against SPEKTRA values (p. 368). Monte Carlo (2 × 10⁵ trials) agrees with GUM/LPU to 0.03 dB (Table 6, p. 367).
- **Interpretation guidance**: A deviation-vs-distance table that is near zero at one distance and grows monotonically with opposite signs on either side (as at 125–250 Hz here) is read as a chamber that "deteriorates gradually" toward its cut-off, not as a discrete reflection; the usable space is defined as the interval where all bands stay inside the ISO 26101 tolerance. Radial (off-axis) checks around the reference point bound the sensitivity to mic placement.
- **Quotable sentences**: "It can be observed in Fig. 4a that in lower-frequency range from 125 to 630 Hz, the deviations between 79 and 89 cm are within ± 1.5 dB limits." (p. 360). "The worst deviation ranging from 0.10 to 0.20 dB from the inverse-square law is taken into account as an error contribution by 'departure from free-field characteristics' inside the defined working space in the measurement frequency range of 125 Hz–20 kHz." (p. 364).

### Ma, Chen, Wang, Liu & Ding 2022, Applied Acoustics 185:108424 — appacoust2022_ma_active_control_large_mppa_point_source.txt

- **Facility / scope**: Method paper, not a chamber study. Problem attacked: low-frequency absorption of a large rectangular micro-perforated panel absorber (MPPA; 0.4 mm holes, 0.5 mm plate, 1 % porosity, 0.08 m cavity, Table 1, p. 4) improved by a loudspeaker acting as a point source in the cavity near the backing wall; modal model of the cavity (modes up to 9 × 9 × 9), plane-wave incidence in a duct; Case 1 panel 0.6 × 0.8 m, Case 2 1.0 × 1.2 m (pp. 2–4). Experiment: MPPA closing one end of a 0.6 × 0.8 m duct, two-microphone transfer-function method (0.2 m spacing, 0.24 m from the panel), Fx-LMS controller, 50–430 Hz in 10 Hz steps (§5, pp. 10–11).
- **Problems observed** (relevant to chambers as the cavity-mode behaviour of a bounded space):
  - Control works only below a cut-off set by the first cavity mode the source can excite: Case 1 modes (0,1,0) 215 Hz, (1,0,0) 287 Hz, (1,1,0) 358 Hz, (0,2,0) 430 Hz; Case 2 143 / 172 / 224 / 287 Hz (Table 3, p. 7). Corner source S1 cuts off at (0,1,0); mid-side S2 at (1,0,0); centre S4 at (0,2,0) — "the highest cutoff frequency of the active MPPA is the resonant frequency of the (0,2,0) cavity mode" (p. 6). "The larger the size of active MPPA is, the narrower the controllable frequency band is." (p. 6).
  - Antisymmetric modes give zero net effect: "the forward sound intensity will be cancelled by the negative sound intensity and the surface net sound intensity will be very small" (p. 5); "The high order cavity mode (except for (0,0,0) mode) has no contribution to sound absorption improvement due to the symmetrical property of their mode shape" (abstract, p. 1).
  - Field uniformity breaks down when wavelength approaches cavity size: at 400 Hz "about 0.86 m ... close to the size of the cavity. Hence, the sound filed radiated by the point source will not be uniform in the cavity" (p. 5); worse for 1.6 × 1.8 m (Fig. 14, p. 10).
  - Duct plane-wave limit: 0.6 × 0.8 m duct cut-off "is only 215 Hz"; centring the primary loudspeaker avoids (0,1), (1,0), (1,1) and extends plane-wave validity to 430 Hz (p. 10). Below 80 Hz the control loudspeaker's response limits performance (p. 12); results "a little undulant" for S1/S3 because the real loudspeaker is not a point source (p. 11).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Place the control source at the cross-section centre (on the nodal lines of (0,1,0), (1,0,0), (1,1,0)); absorption after control is "highly improved and nearly close to 1 in the low frequency range" up to ~400 Hz for Case 1 (p. 4). Error sensing: pressure-release (one mic, cancel cavity pressure) or impedance-matching (two mics / AVS, cancel the reflected plane wave, Eq. 22); IM "slightly better"; sensing point at a cavity corner away from the source and centre — control "significantly weakened when the sensing point is close to the central area" (p. 9). Do not force PR/IM above the cut-off: "there will be no control effect or even the sound absorption coefficient will be reduced" (p. 8).
- **Interpretation guidance**: For a bounded space the first excitable transverse mode, not the wedge/cavity depth, sets the low-frequency limit; whether a mode matters depends on source placement relative to its nodal lines (the same logic that decides which chamber modes a centred source can drive). A control/absorption gain that collapses abruptly at a specific frequency identifies that mode's resonance.
- **Quotable sentences**: "In other words, there is a cutoff frequency for each location of the point source, after which the control effect hardly works immediately and the improvement of the sound absorption is very small." (p. 4). "Relative small sized cavity or low frequency excitation can guarantee the uniformity of the cavity sound field after control, which is conducive to achieve remarkable improvement of sound absorption." (abstract, p. 1).

### Wang, Chen, You, Wu & Qiu 2019, Acta Acustica united with Acustica 105(5):732–742 — actaacustica2019_wang_ucmar_shear_layer_refraction.txt

- **Facility / scope**: Method paper. Problem attacked: shear-layer refraction in open-jet wind tunnels "causes a source shift when estimating the source location with beamforming" (Summary, p. 732). UCMAR gives an explicit (non-iterative) Snell's-law solution for an infinitely thin, convex, non-spreading 3-D shear layer of arbitrary cross-section (elliptic, polygonal, circular, rectangular with fillets), with a sound-speed ratio μ = cs/cr for temperature difference; forward (Eqs. 10, 12) and reverse (Eqs. 14–15) forms; propagation time Eqs. 29–31 feed the DAS steering vector (§2–3, pp. 733–736). Assumptions: "(i) an infinitely thin shear layer with a fixed and convex shape along x-direction; (ii) uniform distribution of velocity and temperature on both sides of the shear layer; and (iii) a negligible effect of reflection and turbulent scattering on the sound propagation" (p. 734).
- **Problems observed**:
  - Refraction moves apparent sources: in the benchmark (circular nozzle D = 0.5 m, M = 0.2, 64-mic array, three monopoles at (0.1, 0, 0.5), (0.1, 0.12, 0.5), (0.1, 0.24, 0.5)) "the located source positions are moved away from the given sources when there is no correction. Moreover, the source near nozzle center are shifted farther downstream" — the effect "further producing distortions rather than a simple source shift" (p. 740; Fig. 9).
  - Simple shift/average-flow corrections are Mach-limited: Padois' shift < 10 % error only up to M = 0.1; Sijtsma's average-flow assumption valid for M < 0.25 (p. 732). Planar corrections neglect rectangular-nozzle corners, "about 3 dB deviation" per Jiao et al. LEE results (p. 732). Rotation-of-coordinates (Bahr et al.) assumes rays bend only along x; polygonal-nozzle rays "viewed in a y-z plane, is not exactly straight lines, which means the assumption of the rotation method ... may produce errors" (p. 737).
  - Temperature across the shear layer matters: hot jet 340.94 K vs 288.15 K ambient (μ = 1.1832) at M = 0.3 — with μ = 1 "the result of UCMAR deflects away from the RT method" and relative propagation-time error grows both up- and downstream (pp. 738; Figs. 5–6).
  - Other shear-layer effects (spectral broadening, vortex scattering, thickness/spreading, turbulent background noise) are acknowledged and explicitly excluded (p. 733); shear-layer thickness is negligible except "far downstream from the nozzle" (pp. 737, 740). Rays near the jet boundary may cross the shear layer twice — source map "discontinuous" near y = 0.25 (p. 740).
  - Beamforming below 2.5 kHz not used "due to the poor resolution of DAS beamforming at low frequencies" (p. 740).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: UCMAR reduces to Amiet/Dobrzynski (1-D), Schlinker–Amiet (planar) and Porteous (convex) forms (§3, pp. 735–736); validated against ray tracing at M = 0.1–0.3 (Fig. 4–5). Cost: 16 s per ray (RT) vs 0.002 s (UCMAR); 3721 × 200 rays ≈ 1 h (RT) vs ≈ 12 s; reverse RT ≈ 100 s vs reverse UCMAR 6 s; whole benchmark 4.8 s (pp. 738, 740). Position error after correction ≤ 0.0102 m for the near-boundary source over 2.5–16 kHz; UCMAR beats RT+DAMAS for the off-axis source at 4–8.3 kHz (p. 740, Fig. 10). Residual error attributed mainly to the beamforming algorithm, not the refraction model (p. 740).
- **Interpretation guidance**: An apparent downstream displacement of beamformed sources that grows toward the jet centreline and with Mach number is shear-layer refraction, not a real source motion; correct the steering-vector propagation time rather than shifting the map. Include temperature (μ ≠ 1) whenever the jet is heated; distrust 1-D/planar corrections near nozzle corners or chamfers.
- **Quotable sentences**: "Consequently, the presence of the shear layer changes the sound in terms of direction, amplitude, and spectra [4] during the acoustic propagation from the jet to the outside microphone arrays." (p. 732). "This phenomenon has also been found by Porteous et al. [30] that proves the refraction effect in a circular wind tunnel further producing distortions rather than a simple source shift." (p. 740).

### Jenny & Anderson 2011, JASA Express Letters 130(2):EL69–EL75 — jasael2011_jenny_anderson_ultrasonic_chamber_qualification.txt

- **Facility / scope**: BYU anechoic chamber, working dimensions 3.57 × 2.88 × 2.59 m, Future Foam 70200YW13 open-cell wedges, 0.032 g/cm³ (2 lb/ft³), "a base, that is, 30.48 cm" (OCR prints "cm2"), alternately oriented (p. - **Problems observed**:
  - Atmospheric absorption: "at ultrasonic frequencies it becomes clearer that atmospheric absorption is an issue ... As the distance increases considerably, ultrasonic qualifications will fall outside of the allowable deviations without atmospheric absorption accounted for. The FRM only qualifies when absorption is accounted for." (p. EL72). Conclusions: absorption must be accounted for "even for distances of only 1 m" (p. EL74).
  - Optimal reference method (ORM) returns non-physical acoustic centres: r0 = 13.7 cm (absorption accounted) and 20.6 cm (not) — "clearly not accurate as the acoustic center cannot be located that far away from the physical surface of the transducer" (pp. EL72–EL73). Same numbers as the thesis.
  - Source directivity: standard allows ±1.5 dB (≤ 630 Hz; OCR garbled), ±2.0 dB (800–5000 Hz), ±2.5 dB (6300–10 000 Hz), ±5.0 dB (> 10 kHz); ka = 0.73 at 6.3 kHz (Hypertweeter) vs 33.7 at 80 kHz (75 kHz transducer). Average deviation from omnidirectionality 22.5 dB (72.2 dB max) without the washer beam blocker, 10.6 dB (38.2 dB max) with it (p. EL74). Same numbers as the thesis.
  - Bands ≥ 63 kHz "commonly yield levels that exceed the allowable ±1.5 dB range with the beam blocker in place"; the 50 kHz band passes but "a couple frequencies bins out of the 201 total bins ... fall below the requirement for 10 dB of signal to noise ratio" (p. EL74). EL70).
- **Solutions / mitigations**: Account for air absorption before averaging; prefer the fixed reference method at ultrasonic frequencies, "perhaps ... improved upon by allowing a user determined offset" (p. EL73); aim directional sources at each traverse; washer beam blocker 3–4 cm in front of the transducer; if the chamber will only be used with directional sources, qualify with such sources "as long as this is understood when reporting data" (p. EL74).
- **Interpretation guidance**: Check that any fitted acoustic-centre offset r0 is physically plausible before trusting an ORM "pass"; a fit that needs a large r0 is absorbing absorption or reflection into the source model. Up-and-down swings along a traverse are interference, but their true amplitude is unknown when the spacing is many wavelengths.
- **Differences from the BYU thesis version**: none in the numbers — r0 = 13.7 / 20.6 cm, 22.5 dB (72.2 max) / 10.6 dB (38.2 max), 7.3 λ / 9.2 λ / 1.03 mm, ±1.5 dB tolerance all identical. - **Quotable sentences**: "In the ultrasonic frequency range, anechoic chamber qualification requires that atmospheric absorption be accounted for even for distances of only 1 m." (p. EL74).
