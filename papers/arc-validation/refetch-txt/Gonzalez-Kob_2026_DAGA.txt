Influence of room acoustics on directivity measurement of loudspeakers
Rubén González1 , Malte Kob2
Erich Thienhaus Institute, Detmold University of Music, 32760 Detmold, Germany.
Email: 1 ruben.gonzalez@stud.hfm-detmold.de; 2 malte.kob@hfm-detmold.de

Abstract
The directivity of loudspeakers and music instruments
are often made available in form of polar diagrams,
balloons or pressure isobar plots. These visualisations
are usually based upon measurements made in acoustically anechoic conditions which are not easily available
outside acoustic labs. This study evaluates the effect
of the reverberant field and reflections in four different rooms on swept sine measurements for directivity
acquisition of two high-end loudspeakers following the
AES56-2008 procedure with guidelines for loudspeaker
directivity measurements. Comparisons of Frequency
Response Functions (FRF), as well as polar diagrams are
compared to 3D measurements with a resolution of 5° in
an anechoic chamber. The results show how much the
loudspeaker radiation is affected above and below the
critical distance and by reflections. Moreover, for the
case of the non-anechoic field conditions, SDM analysis
were used to track the direction of incidence of image
sources that impact on the directivity evaluation.

Introduction
For loudspeaker directivity measurements using polar
representations, the AES 56-2008 standard [1] defines
the requirements necessary to ensure data accuracy and
measurement repeatability. However, this standard does
not specify the room acoustic conditions for measurements performed at typical angular resolution (5 · 5°).
As a result, measurements conducted in non-anechoic
environments may produce significantly different polar
representations along both horizontal and vertical axes,
even when identical equipment is used. This variation
is caused by discrete reflections, diffraction, and diffuse
reflections that follow the direct sound from the source at
the measurement position. The impact of such deviation
from free field conditions has been investigated earlier
[2, 3, 4].
This study initially examines the differences in averaged
sound pressure levels obtained from impulse response
(IR) measurements along the horizontal axis, compared
to reference measurements in anechoic conditions. The
aim is to determine how reverberation time, room volume, and the resulting critical distance influence directivity polar diagrams. To achieve this, directivity
measurements were conducted in four rooms with different acoustic characteristics (see Table 1). The critical
distance for a diffuse reverberant field approximation is
calculated using the following expression [5]:

Experimental Setup
The omnidirectional microphone NTi model M2010 with
an input sensitivity of 35 mV/Pa at 1 kHz was used in
conjunction with an RME Babyface Pro for D/A, and
A/D conversion of both the excitation signal and the
measured impulse response of the system respectively
[7]. The exponential sine sweep used as excitation signal
had a length t = 0.5 seconds, and a sampling rate
fs = 44.1 kHz. Therefore, the fft degree defined as 15,
i.e. the next integer above log2 (fs ∗ t) = 14.428.
To obtain energy levels within octave frequency bands,
all measured impulse responses were windowed and convolved with corresponding band-pass filters. A time
window of 1024 samples with an overlap factor of 0.25
was applied for this processing step.
Figure 1 shows how the Neumann KH120A loudspeaker
was placed over a turntable model Stage Banner 10AT.
Special care was taken for the correct centering of the
point of rotation and measurement axis. For the rotatory

In all the rooms the noise levels were measured following
the Noise Rating curves (ISO 1996), using a low noise
microphone Type 47HC from GRAS Sound & Vibration
with an input sensitivity calibrated at 335.7 mV/Pa.
An array of six intensity microphones Type 40AI from

Results and Analysis
Directivity measurements in the anechoic chamber at
KIO were conducted along both horizontal and vertical
axes, enabling full three-dimensional directivity visualization. Due to the very low reverberation time values,
these measurements are effectively limited to near field
conditions. Figure 2 presents 3D directivity balloons at
approximately 1 kHz and 2 kHz with the measurement
axis MA=0° pointing upwards. While both exhibit quasicardioid radiation patterns, the higher frequency case
shows an additional lobes above and below the loudspeaker enclosure caused by diffraction effects.
Figure 3 presents normalized polar diagrams for both
near and far field measurements. In the reverberant hall
and laboratory at ETI, significant differences in sound
pressure levels are observed, particularly for frequencies
of 1 kHz and above, within the MA range from 150° to
180°. This indicates that, at the rear of the loudspeaker,
both near and far field measurements are influenced
by early and late reflections from surrounding surfaces,
which contribute to the averaged pressure energy.

References

In this study, a simplified definition of critical distance
based on a diffuse sound field assumption was used
to determine near and far field measurement positions,
leading to varying results. In the anechoic room at ETI,
the averaged sound pressure level decreases proportionally with distance across the entire frequency spectrum.
However, when the same distance criteria are applied in
the ETI laboratory, pressure level reductions of up to
18 dB occur at specific frequencies.

Several additional factors beyond critical distance influence loudspeaker directivity measurements at standard
angular resolution. These include reflections from the
turntable, early reflections from walls, ambient noise, and
the orientation of the measurement axis relative to the
room geometry. Consequently, changing the position of
both the rotating source and the receiver with respect to
reflective surfaces can alter the extent to which these factors affect the resulting polar directivity representation,
even if the same critical distance is maintained.
