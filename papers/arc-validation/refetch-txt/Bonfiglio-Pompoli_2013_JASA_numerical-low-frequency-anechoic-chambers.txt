This paper presents a description of the use of simplified numerical methodologies for the
optimization of the low cut-off frequency of anechoic and hemi-anechoic chambers. The anechoic
chamber is modeled as a cavity with proper surface impedance boundary conditions. First, the
shape of the wedges is optimized by means of a minimization-based procedure of a finite element
model of such elements in a “virtual” impedance tube for a plane wave field. An equivalent surface
impedance of the wedges is determined from those data. An analytical procedure is then used
to determine the complex reflection coefficient for spherical waves at oblique incidence. Finally,
a complex image source approach is used to predict the sound field within the chamber. The
methodology is applied to two anechoic chambers and the results are compared in terms of sound
decay along fixed directions and surface pressure distributions.

qualification of an anechoic chamber. In fact, such qualification has to be carried out according to ISO Standard 3745
Annex A1 by determining the deviation from inverse square
law in different directions from a point sound source. As
a consequence, the prediction of the performance of the
chamber requires the simulation of its internal sound field.
However, the complexity of the absorbing systems used in
those chambers (generally wedge-shaped) makes the complete simulation of the sound field difficult when using multidomain models, unless high computational resources are used
(i.e., finite and boundary element methods).11 Moreover from
previous research,12 it has been shown that a simplified finite
element model (parallelepiped-shaped chamber with proper
surface impedance boundary conditions) cannot be used to
predict the internal sound field. The main reason for this is
due to the use of plane wave surface impedance for the boundaries (which is directly obtained from simulation in a virtual
impendence tube) when the sound field is still spherical
(usually at very low frequencies).
In this paper a simplified analytical approach based on
complex source image propagation for spherical waves is
presented and predictions are compared to results obtained
in two real anechoic chambers.
The paper is organized as follows. The description of
the methodology is given in the following section. The third
section will report the results for both the optimal shape of
the wedges and internal sound field prediction. Finally, concluding remarks will be given in the last section.
II. DESCRIPTION OF THE METHODOLOGY
A. Optimization of the shape of the wedges

The procedure for the optimization of the shape of the
wedges is based on a dynamic finite element model, in the
sense that the geometry of the elements is changed at each
iteration according to a non-linear best-fit scheme that maximizes their normal incidence sound absorption coefficient.

The design of anechoic chambers is of increasing importance since such rooms are suitable for the measurement of
radiated noise from a source. To this end, reference should
be made to ISO Standard 3745,1 which describes the procedure for determining sound power within such chambers.
However, this standard provides few general indications as
to the design of the wedges. As a function of the minimum
working frequency of the chamber (cut-off frequency), the
wedge length is indicated as being equal to a quarter of the
wavelength (k/4 rule hereafter) and the absorption coefficient at normal incidence is higher than 0.99 for all frequencies above the cut-off limit.
In this paper, two different aspects related to the optimal
design of anechoic chambers are discussed and analyzed,
including (1) the three-dimensional optimization of the
shape of the wedges and (2) the simulation of the sound field
within the chamber itself.
The optimal shape of the wedges has been widely investigated both experimentally2–4 and numerically.5–9 In particular, Lee et al.10 proposed an optimal shape design, through
a systematic design method based on an iterative optimization process of a two-dimensional poro-elastic acoustical
foam.
This research presents a further step in the design of
three-dimensional wedges by means of a completely automated procedure through the combination of a finite element
model and a non-linear best-fit approach. The analyses are
limited to rigid frame porous materials; however, the methodology is general and can thus be extended to poro-elastic
media.

Once the optimal geometry has been chosen, it is possible to further minimize the overall dimensions of the wedges
within the anechoic chamber by identifying the maximum
volume of material that can be removed and truncating the
wedges along planes parallel to their base area, still maintaining condition an > 0.99. In particular, starting from the
geometry that minimizes the total length of the wedges (that
is lw ¼ 0.59 m, h ¼ 0.24 m, and B ¼ 0.52 m), additional FEM
simulations have been carried out by considering a reduction
of 10%, 15%, and 20% with respect to the total length of the
wedge. Figure 4 shows comparison between such solutions
in terms of the normal incidence sound absorption coefficient. From the figure it is possible to observe that a truncation of 15% of the length, which corresponds to a reduction

of the total length of the wedge equal to 12 cm, still guarantees the condition indicated by standard ISO Standard 3745.
The methodology here proposed is general and can be
extended to other models for predicting acoustical properties
of materials. As an example additional optimization is carried out by using the Delany and Bazley model13 (a general
semi-empirical model that can be applied to several fibrous
materials) as a function of the airflow resistivity variation. In
particular, six values of airflow resistivity are investigated
including: 0.3, 0.46, 15, 26, 35, and 40 kPa s /m2. In addition,
the best solutions of the geometrical parameters for a cut-off
frequency of 100 Hz are collected in a way that is possible to
build, by means of regression analyses, the optimal shape of
the wedges for any possible value of airflow resistivity. The
values of the total length of the wedge (lw þ h), the number
of wedges per unit area 1/B2, and the ratio between h and lw
are depicted in Figs. 5–7, together with the related regression
curves.
The analysis of Figs. 5–7 indicates that when the airflow
resistivity increases the optimal wedges tend to be longer
and thinner. The values of geometrical parameters for all the
values of airflow resistivity are summarized in Table II.
Finally, it is worthy of mention that Fig. 5 shows that the k/4
rule is not always respected; in fact for airflow resistivity
higher than 10 kPa s=m2 , a wedge length greater than 85 cm
is required (which corresponds to a quarter of the wavelength for a cut-off frequency of 100 Hz).

Numerical data were calculated by using a complex image

and numerical data were interpolated by using the MATLAB 4

distributions.

IV. CONCLUSIONS

This paper reports the results of a numerical design of
sound absorbing wedges as well as a numerical study for
determining the cut-off frequency of anechoic chambers.
Results of the predictions have been compared with experimental tests carried out in real hemi-anechoic and anechoic
chambers and the results have been found to be quite satisfactory in terms of sound decay along fixed directions and
spatial distributions.
