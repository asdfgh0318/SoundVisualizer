This study proposes an alternative to the classic wedge design for anechoic chambers,
which is the uniform-then-gradient, ﬂat-wall (UGFW) structure. The working mechanisms
of the proposed structure and the traditional wedge are analyzed. It is found that their
absorption patterns are different. The parameters of both structures are optimized for
achieving minimum absorber depth, under the condition of absorbing 99% of normal
incident sound energy. It is found that, the UGFW structure achieves a smaller total depth
for the cut-off frequencies ranging from 100 Hz to 250 Hz. This paper also proposes a
modiﬁcation for the complex source image (CSI) model for the empirical simulation of
anechoic chambers, originally proposed by Bonﬁglio et al. [J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 134 (1), 285–
291 (2013)]. The modiﬁed CSI model considers the non-locally reactive effect of absorbers
at oblique incidence, and the improvement is veriﬁed by a full, ﬁnite-element simulation
of a small chamber. With the modiﬁed CSI model, the performance of both decorations
with the optimized parameters in a large chamber is simulated. The simulation results are
analyzed and checked against the tolerance of 1.5 dB deviation from the inverse square
law, stipulated in the ISO standard 3745(2003). In terms of the total decoration depth and
anechoic chamber performance, the UGFW structure is better than the classic wedge
design.

1. Introduction
In 1946, Beranek and Sleeper [1] published their seminal paper on the design and construction of anechoic chambers. In
that paper, they investigated ﬁve categories of structures, or six kinds, as decorated structures inside the anechoic chamber
to absorb at least 99% of normal incident sound energy. They showed that the most effective decoration was the wedge
structure, which achieved the minimum total absorber depth required. Since then, the wedge structure has become the
classic and most widely used conﬁguration. Beranek and Sleeper's [1] wedges were made by ﬁberglass, and the geometric
optimization was conducted through the testing of numerous combinations. Koidan et al. [2] reported a “hybrid” wedge
design, consisting of two kinds of glass wools with different densities, but the geometric parameters were also determined
by experimentation.
It was only after the development of numerical methods that accurate wedge property calculation and its geometric
design became possible. There is still no analytical solution for the room acoustic problem with wedges. Easwaran and

Munjal [3] proposed a ﬁnite-element method (FEM) model of the wedge, and the result matched well with data obtained
from impedance-tube testing. Wang et al. [4] investigated the wedge properties with a boundary element method (BEM). In

wedge numerically. In 2013, Bonﬁglio et al. [6] modeled the wedge in a virtual impedance tube with a FEM model, and
s
optimized its parameters with a minimization procedure in MATLAB . Lee et al. [7] obtained an unusual shape through
topology optimization for normal incidence, and its acoustic property was better than ordinary shaped wedges, theoretically. However, no experiment was conducted to verify the ﬁndings.
In addition to the wedge structure, some alternatives of the decoration in anechoic chambers have also been investigated, experimented and manufactured, one of which is the multi-layer, ﬂat-wall (MLFW) design. Actually, the idea dates
back to 1936 when Bedell [8] proposed a design of 16 cloth layers to achieve 98% or 99% normal incidence absorption. With
today's advanced manufacturing capability and the need to save space, the interest in the MLFW design has been rekindled,
e.g. Davern [9] in 1980. Subsequently, Dunn and Davern [10] developed a transfer matrix method to calculate the performance of such a multi-layered system, and the result matched well with the experiment. Around 2005, Xu et al. optimized
the multi-layered absorber with evolutionary algorithm [11,12] and constructed an anechoic chamber with polyurethane
foams using 16 kinds of available materials [13]. Besides the usual single porosity porous material, a double porosity porous
material has also been proposed. In 2001, Atalla et al. pointed out that the macro-perforated porous material could enhance
the low frequency absorption [14]. Then Sgard et al. did a synthetic review of the existing models for the double porosity
material and gave some practical suggestions for choosing parameters [15]. Moreover, Becot et al. applied the double
porosity theory to predict the performance of irregular-shaped absorbers, including the wedge structure [16].
In addition to porous material (e.g. ﬁberglass, foam), micro-perforated panel (MPP) can also be used. Chang et al. (2005)
optimized a single-layer sound absorption material accompanied by one layer of MPP by genetic algorithm and mathematical gradient methods [17] under the restriction of limited thickness. Apart from the combination of common absorption
materials (e.g. ﬁberglass, foam, MPP), the sintered ﬁbrous metals are fabricated and tested. Meng et al. investigated the
sound absorption performance of multi-layered sintered ﬁbrous metal with gradient parameters [18].
The MLFW design has the advantage of less complexity and being easier for installation. Therefore, this study investigates
a limit case of the MLFW structure, which consists of a material with a continuous distribution of ﬂow resistivity, called
gradient ﬂat-wall structure. When the gradient structure has a section of constant ﬂow resistivity at the front, it is called a
uniform-then-gradient ﬂat-wall (UGFW) structure. The purpose of proposing the UGFW structure is to quickly optimize the
distribution of material parameters. For real manufacturing, it is not necessary to manufacture the exact gradient structure.
A few layers of constant-property material also work. In the following section, a 10-layers result is demonstrated to be good
enough to realize the gradient effect. It is found that an optimized UGFW design performs slightly better than the optimized
classic wedge. Details of the sound absorption and reﬂection characteristics are investigated to reveal the precise
mechanism of such performance gain.
It would be ideal if sound propagation in an anechoic chamber can be simulated by a full ﬁnite-element scheme without
any simpliﬁcation. However, such simulation demands excessive computational resource for the time being. As an alternative, the boundary element method (BEM) can be used by replacing the wedge structure with a local boundary admittance condition. However, as Schneider [19] pointed out, this scheme may be inaccurate without considering the non-locally
reactive effect. Actually, before the advent of the modern computer, Bedell [8] proposed an acoustic-ray model in 1936. In
this model, the sound inside the chamber is decomposed into direct sound and multiple reﬂected sounds. This idea was
applied and modiﬁed by Zha et al. [20] and Bonﬁglio et al. [6] to predict the anechoic chamber's property. In Bonﬁglio et al.'s
[6] model, the method is called complex source image (CSI) model. However, both Zha et al. [20] and Bonﬁglio et al. [6] only
considered the wedge to be a locally reactive boundary at oblique incidence, which may be inappropriate when the decoration is the wedge structure, as its absorption property is obviously dependent on the incident angle. Therefore, a
modiﬁed CSI model is presented in the current study to account for the non-locally reactive effect of the classic wedge
structure and the proposed UGFW structure.
As to the design standard of the anechoic chamber, the most widely used is the minimum of 99% sound energy
absorption in the ISO standard 3745(2003) [21]. The 99% standard requires that the normal-incidence sound energy
absorption coefﬁcient of the wall and the ceiling should be larger than 99% in the speciﬁed frequency range, in which the
lowest value is called the cut-off frequency. This sound absorption coefﬁcient is measured in an impedance tube. In real
situation of calibrating an anechoic chamber, as mentioned by Beranek and Sleeper [1], there are at least three kinds of
methods. However, the simplest and the most widely used is the inverse square law method [22,23], which is also adopted
by the ISO standard 3745(2003) [21]. In the ISO standard, different limitations on the deviation from the strict inverse square
law are proposed for different chamber types and different working frequency ranges. In this paper, the 1.5 dB deviation
from the inverse square law is adopted. However, the design standard and the calibration standard are deﬁned in different
situations. One is for the absorption of normal incident plane wave inside a tube, and the other is for the absorption of
spherical waves inside a chamber. In this paper, the wedge and UGFW structures are both optimized under the 99%
absorption standard in an impedance tube, and their performance inside a chamber is simulated with the modiﬁed CSI
model and compared with the 1.5 dB deviation standard.
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces the simulation and optimization methods of the
wedge and UGFW structures in normal incidence with ﬁnite-element model, and the modiﬁcation of the CSI model.

2.2. Optimization of parameters
In the design of an anechoic chamber, the free space for acoustic measurement in a given allocated lab space is
important. It is desirable for decorations to occupy as little space (depth) as possible, while satisfying the 99% absorption
standard in ISO standard 3745(2003) [21]. Therefore, a constraint condition of α Z 99% is imposed on the optimization
s
procedure. In this paper, sequential quadratic programming (SQP) algorithm in MATLAB is applied. The detailed optimization settings are shown in Table 1. The calculation frequency range is chosen to be [fc, 5fc], where fc is the design cut-off
frequency. The performance at frequencies higher than 5fc is considered to be adequate.

2.3. Simulation of 3D acoustic ﬁeld over a periodic array of wedges
To simulate the acoustic ﬁeld inside an anechoic chamber, a realistic three-dimensional (3D) wedge is used. To begin
with, these wedges are assumed to line up in a periodic array; and one such module is shown in Fig. 4. The calculation
domain contains four staggered 3D wedges, and the parameters are the same as that of 2D model. The incident wave
approaches the module at an oblique angle, and the method proposed by Wang et al. [27] to handle such conﬁguration is
adopted. The governing equation is the Helmholtz Equation, and the boundary condition for the bottom is the hard wall
condition. The boundary condition for the four sides are periodic boundary conditions, which requires the pressure and
particle velocity on the two opposite sides to be periodic, with a constant phase angle difference dictated by the incident
wave. The boundary condition for the incident (top) plane is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) boundary condition [28] to
ensure that all modes of the reﬂected wave leave freely. The implementation of DtN boundary condition is listed in the
Appendix A (more detailed implementation information can be found in Wang et al. [27]).
The method of simulation for the oblique incidence for the UGFW structure is the same as that of the 3D wedge
structure. However, the result of UGFW structure is independent of the azimuth angle β and only needs to be run once for all
elevation angles.
In Zha et al.'s paper [20], the method of virtual source image was introduced to simulate anechoic chamber. A lot of
simpliﬁcations and assumptions were made. For example, only the primary reﬂection was considered, and locally reactive
property was assumed for the wedge. In Bonﬁglio et al.'s paper [6], the method is called complex source image (CSI) method,
and multiple reﬂections were considered, with the highest order of reﬂection determined by the convergence criterion. The
wedge was also assumed to be a locally reactive impedance surface. The assumption is removed in the present study as the
acoustic property of a wedge is obviously non-locally reactive, considering its irregular shape.

3. Results and analysis
3.1. Optimization results and analysis
The two structures are optimized to ﬁnd the minimized total depth with the method described in the previous section.
The result is displayed in Fig. 7. It is clearly shown that, the minimum depths of the UGFW structure are smaller than those
of the classic wedge for a wide range of cut-off frequency from 100 Hz to 250 Hz.
At this stage, the primary concern is the total depth. However, the reduction of the total depth does not necessarily
translate into a reduction of the total volume of materials used. Actually, the volume of material required is increased. The
cost of the increased use of material may be partly compensated for by the ease of installation compared with the
classical wedge.
The proposed UGFW structure has smaller total depth than the wedge structure. Further analysis shows that their
absorption mechanisms of the two structures are different.

The sound energy absorption rate for the two structures is shown in Fig. 9.
For the wedge structure at 100 Hz, there is a sharp absorption peak around the base of the wedge in the energy
absorption rate curve, and the absorption rate elsewhere is relatively small. The phenomenon is caused by the unique
geometry of the wedge. When sound hits the two edges of the wedge, most of the sound is bounced back to the air space
between the wedges. In such low frequency range, the normal impedance of the porous material is much larger than air; the
impedance mismatch is large even when the factor of incident angle is considered. Therefore, little sound goes into the
wedge along the interface between the air and the wedge. This phenomenon is also shown in Fig. 10(a) by the sound
intensity distribution at 100 Hz. The sound intensity vector at the air-wedge interface is almost parallel to the interface, and
the sound intensity inside the wedge is almost zero. Therefore, the absorption rate of sound energy by the wedge front is
very small. Meanwhile, the sound intensity in the air domain increases greatly due to the trapping of sound towards the
corner at the base of the wedge.
For the wedge structure at 300 Hz, the sharp absorption peak disappears, while the width of efﬁcient absorption part is
broadened to the front. At this relatively high frequency, the impedance of porous material decreases, leading to better
match with that of air. Therefore, more sound energy goes into the wedge along the interface, as shown in Fig. 10(b), which
broadens the absorption band.
As shown by the open circles in Fig. 9(a) and (b), the UGFW structure has a broad absorption pattern in the depth
direction and a peak at the front. This broad pattern is also shown in Fig. 11(a) and (b) for intensity. Most sound energy goes
into the porous material through the air-absorber interface, and gets dissipated by the porous material gradually. The peak
absorption rate occurs inside the uniform part of the UGFW structure, which indicates that the front uniform part with small
ﬂow resistivity acts not only as an impedance transition, but also as an efﬁcient absorber.

3.2. Simulation results for the anechoic chamber
The modiﬁed CSI model is used to simulate the performance of the anechoic chamber with the two decoration structures
designed for the cut-off frequency of 100 Hz. The optimized parameters are given in Table 2. The simulated chamber is
9 m  8 m  7 m (wedge tip to wedge tip). The point source with unit amplitude is put at the center of the chamber, and the
ﬁrst testing line ranges from the center to one of the eight vertices. The second testing line is from the center of the chamber
to the center of the closest wall. According to the ISO standard 3745(2003) [21], the curve of sound pressure level (SPL)
attenuation in the anechoic chamber should satisfy the inverse square law within a tolerance of 71.5 dB. The chamber
performance is not considered to be anechoic when the tolerance is breached. As shown below, this anechoic condition is
usually not satisﬁed for the entire testing line for the design cut-off frequency as deviation grows in the region close to the
wedges. Xu et al. [13] considers the chamber performance to be acceptable if at least 75% of the length of the testing line
conforms to the tolerance limit of 71.5 dB around the strict inverse square law. This 75%-length criterion is also adopted in
the current study to determine the cut-off frequency of the whole chamber. The frequency above which at least 75% of the
testing line length satisﬁes the inverse square law to within 1.5 dB deviation is therefore deﬁned as the effective cut-off
frequency. As shown below, this effective cut-off frequency is usually higher (worse) than the design cut-off frequency for
which the normal incidence sound energy absorption rate is equal or above 99%.

4. Conclusions
To investigate the design of anechoic chambers, two types of interior chamber decorations are modeled and analyzed.
They are the most common wedge structure, and its alternative, the uniform-gradient ﬂat-wall (UGFW) structures. In
addition, a modiﬁcation of the complex source image (CSI) method is proposed to simulate sound propagation in anechoic
chambers, by considering the non-locally reactive effect of the absorbers. The following conclusions are drawn.
