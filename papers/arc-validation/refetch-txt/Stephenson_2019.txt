An experimental campaign was undertaken to identify the effects of flow recirculation on an isolated rotor’s acoustic emissions in a closed anechoic chamber. It is shown that flow recirculation
results in a significant increase in higher harmonic noise, with an increase of more than 15 dB in
some harmonics. This increase in noise is due to the nature of testing in a closed facility and does
not represent the acoustic emissions of a similar rotor in hovering flight outdoors. Future measurements of rotors and full unmanned aerial vehicles in closed anechoic chambers must acknowledge
recirculation effects and attempt to mitigate the effect on reported measurements.

II. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

Unmanned aerial systems (UAS) are becoming prevalent
within the commercial, private, and government sectors. With the
rise in such systems comes the natural concern over noise pollution caused by these vehicles. Research in UAS acoustics is a
nascent field, with many researchers beginning to identify and
model the aeroacoustic emissions of these vehicles.1–3 Much of
the research in this field relies heavily on the past work of
researchers investigating larger systems, such as helicopter and
propeller aircraft.4,5 However, some of this knowledge is institutionalized and not properly documented in the literature, which
results in new researchers to the field being unaware of common
pitfalls to aeroacoustic testing of propeller and rotorcraft vehicles.
One critical piece of institutionalized knowledge regards
the effect of flow recirculation when testing rotating systems in
anechoic chambers. It is known that the acoustic emissions of
rotating systems are primarily a result of the rotor blades moving through the fluid medium (thickness noise) and imposing a
force on the fluid medium (loading noise). As the rotating system generates thrust, this results in the movement of the fluid. In
a closed chamber, this downwash recirculates around the chamber and is ingested by the rotating system. Ingesting the turbulent, recirculated flow imposes a new, unsteady load on the
rotor system resulting in a modification to the loading noise
emitted by the system. This flow recirculation, which is a function of rotor size, thrust, and the size of the facility, is not experienced by a rotor system hovering in free flight, outside of a
confined environment. Therefore, if the intent of a research
campaign is to model a rotor system or full vehicle hovering
outside in quiescent conditions, it is imperative that the effects
of flow recirculation are mitigated.

The effect of flow recirculation on a UAS rotor is demonstrated using a single rotor in hover, within NASA
Langley Research Center’s Small Hover Anechoic Chamber
(SHAC, formerly called the Small Anechoic Jet Facility).
The employed rotor is a two-bladed, fixed pitch propeller,
9.4 in. in diameter. The SHAC measures 12.70  8.40  10.70
(L  W  H) from wedge-tip to wedge-tip. This chamber,
shown schematically in Fig. 1 and pictorially in Fig. 2, has an
inlet and outlet to facilitate flow through the chamber. For this
experiment, the facility was operated in a fully anechoic mode
by plugging both ports with acoustically absorbent material. A
subset of data was acquired without the inlet and exhaust
plugged, which resulted in a negligible difference to the flow
recirculation effects seen below.
Six, 1/4 in. free-field type 4939 B&K microphones,
defined as M1–M6 in Fig. 1, were used to acquire acoustic
emissions from the rotor at various elevation angles (h). An
azimuthal array was not employed as the acoustic emissions
of an isolated rotor in hover should be symmetric. The
microphone locations are provided in Table I, measured axially relative to the rotor plane, radially from the rotor hub,
and provided in elevation angles.
An ATI-IA Mini40, six degree of freedom load cell was
employed to capture relevant loads data. A laser tachometer
was also used to provide real-time tracking of rotor position and
rotation rate (RPM). All data were sampled simultaneously.

At approximately 7.5 s into the run, flow recirculation in
the room has established and the higher harmonics of the
BPF are now observable. The amplitudes of the higher harmonic content have increased in excess of 15 dB for many of
the harmonics. This higher harmonic content is not indicative of what a rotor in hover outdoor would emit, but is
instead an artifact of testing within the confined space of an
anechoic chamber. The thrust loads are also sensitive to the
presence of flow recirculation, however the mean thrust
remains constant. This strongly suggests that the flow recirculation causes an unsteady loading on the rotor system
which results in the increased harmonic content.
This unsteady loading postulate can be tested by investigating some of the other microphones within the anechoic
chamber. Unsteady loading noise of a rotor is known to
emit predominantly above and below the rotor plane, while
thickness noise is predominantly emitted in the plane of the
rotor. So, if we investigate a microphone above and below
the rotor, as well as a microphone in the plane of the rotor,
we should see the effect of the recirculation in the out of
plane microphones more prominently than in the in-plane
microphone.
Figure 4 includes spectra calculated using a half second of data centered around 5 and 8 s into the run. These
timestamps correspond to a pre-recirculation and postrecirculation regime, as shown in Fig. 3. The three microphones shown are from above the plane of the rotor (M1),
in the plane of the rotor (M4), and below the plane of the
rotor (M6).
It can be seen from Fig. 4, that the first harmonic of
the blade passage frequency is relatively unaffected by the
flow recirculation within the room. However, the second
harmonic for the out of plane microphones has increased
by approximately 30 dB. The third blade passage harmonic
has increased by more than 15 dB, with higher harmonics
having also experienced significant increases in the acoustic amplitudes. The relative steadiness in levels for preand post-recirculation for the in-plane microphone is of
particular note and lends credence to the postulation that
the unsteady load caused by flow recirculation is the cause
for the increased noise in the out of plane microphone
measurements.
