Flight Conditions in an Anechoic Environment for
Spectral and Psychoacoustic Characterization

Abstract In drone noise research, source directivity represents the missing link between
near-source sound generation and community-relevant noise exposure. Accurate directivity
information is essential for approximating far-field sound propagation and predicting noise
impact under realistic operational conditions, and it is important for physically plausible auralisations. However, determining drone directivities is experimentally non-trivial: indoor measurements are typically constrained by limited space, whereas outdoor campaigns introduce
substantial uncertainties due to weather-driven variability, interfering ambient sound, and
ground-reflection artefacts. This study presents a measurement campaign in which multirotor UAVs of different sizes, with dimensions of up to 2.6 m (measured from rotor tip to rotor
tip), were investigated in an anechoic chamber under realistic flight conditions. A spatially
distributed array of 64 microphones was deployed throughout the chamber, surrounding the
sources, and enabling measurements of free hovering as well as vertical and forward flight
manoeuvres. Drone positions during flight were tracked using a LiDAR scanner. For the
evaluation, a backpropagation method was employed to obtain time signals for a defined
distance around the drones. In addition to sound pressure level directivity characteristics,
sound quality metrics were calculated to complement the analyses by describing the psychoacoustic characteristics of the emission pattern. Selected results for two UAVs will be
discussed. The measurement data will serve as input for emission signal synthesis and
source directivity modelling, enabling physically plausible drone auralisations for noise impact assessment.
Keywords: UAV Directivity; Anechoic Chamber Measurement; Psychoacoustics

1.

I NTRODUCTION

Research on unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), often also referred to as drones, has gained
increasing attention as their use in civil applications is expected to grow and noise remains a
key challenge for public acceptance (Rascon & Martinez-Carranza, 2024). While UAVs are
not necessarily louder than other urban traffic or aircraft noise sources, their tonal components, temporal fluctuations, and manoeuvre-dependent sound signatures can make them
particularly annoying (Rascon & Martinez-Carranza, 2024; Kawai et al., 2024). This motivates the use of psychoacoustic metrics in addition to conventional sound pressure levels,
particularly when the perceptual relevance of different flight conditions or vehicle configurations is to be assessed. Although field studies are becoming increasingly feasible (Green,
Lotinga, & Torija, 2025), regulatory constraints still limit systematic investigations of UAV
flight scenarios under realistic urban conditions. Drone noise research therefore continues
to rely on simulation-based prediction to study noise exposure caused by different flight conditions, UAV configurations, and operational scenarios. When combined with auralisation,
such simulations also enable perceptual assessments of scenarios that would be difficult or
impractical to investigate comprehensively in field experiments.
For most modelling approaches that are not fully physics-based, a plausible source description requires both a source signal and accurate directivity information (Müller, 2026).
This is particularly relevant for drones, which often exhibit pronounced acoustic directivity
(Herold, 2022; Wunderli, Meister, Boolakee, & Heutschi, 2022; Zhou, Jiang, & Huang, 2022)
that affects predicted sound levels, spectral characteristics, and, in perceptual experiments,
the realism of auralised scenarios.
While first steps towards standardized UAV noise measurements have been made (ISO,
2024), drone directivity measurements are not yet well established and remain experimentally challenging, particularly because realistic flight conditions, source motion, and environmental constraints are difficult to capture in a controlled and reproducible manner. Directivity
measurements require free-field conditions, limiting suitable environments to outdoor test
sites or anechoic facilities. For UAVs, outdoor measurements allow realistic operation but
require large test areas and are susceptible to ambient noise, ground reflections, wind, and
weather, which can affect both measurement data and the UAV operating state, including
rotor speeds and the resulting acoustic signature. Anechoic indoor measurements provide
greater acoustic control but are constrained by available space and safety requirements,
particularly for larger UAVs or realistic flight manoeuvres.
This study investigates the acoustic and psychoacoustic directivity of two UAVs differing
substantially in size, configuration, and acoustic signature. Measurements were conducted
in an anechoic chamber using a spatially distributed microphone array, enabling directivity
analysis under controlled free-field conditions while retaining realistic operation. In addition
to hovering, the measurement setup allowed slow forward flight and vertical manoeuvres.

M ETHOD

2.1

Drones

The measurement campaign included several very different UAVs, two of which are investigated in this paper: the Holybro X500, a medium-sized commercial quadcopter, and
UBADRON, a custom-built hexacopter developed at the Chair of Flight Guidance and Air
Transport at TU Berlin. The X500 has a wheelbase of 50 cm, 25.4 cm rotors, and a take-off
mass of 1.96 kg in the investigated configuration, slightly below its maximum take-off mass
of 2.11 kg. UBADRON is considerably larger, with a wheelbase of 158.7 cm, a rotor diameter of 72.8 cm, an overall tip-to-tip width of approximately 2.6 m, and a take-off mass of
12 kg during the measurements, compared with a maximum take-off mass of 25 kg. Due to

its size, UBADRON was measured only in hover, whereas the X500 was additionally investigated in forward flight as well as slow ascent and descent manoeuvres. Both drones are

2.2

Measurement Setup

Figure 1: Image of the measurement setup with safety net constructions in the anechoic room (left). Measured
Drones (right): Holybro X500 with attached retroreflector (upper) and custom built UBADRON (lower).

The measurement campaign was conducted in the fully anechoic room of the Engineering Acoustics Lab at TU Berlin, with dimensions of 13.5 m, 8.3 m, and 7.4 m (L × W × H)
and a lower cut-off frequency of 63 Hz. For safety reasons, a heavy-duty net was suspended
in the centre of the room to protect researchers, pilots, and laboratory equipment in case of
loss of drone control. The protected flight volume was further extended in the x-direction
by an additional safety net and was bounded vertically by the walkable Kevlar mesh floor,
resulting in a usable flight volume of approximately 10 m × 6 m × 3.5 m. The measurement
setup is shown in Figure 1.
A set of 64 1/4” GRAS 40PK measurement microphones was deployed throughout the
chamber and connected to a data acquisition system that recorded all 64 channels simultaneously as sound pressure signals pm [n] at a sampling rate of fs = 102.4 kHz. As illustrated
in Figure 2, vertical microphone arrays were mounted on the room wedges near the centre
of each wall, spanning more than 4 m in height with slight lateral offsets between individual microphones. Additional microphones were placed near the room centre on the Kevlar
mesh floor and in the overhead safety net, some of which, together with one of the lateral wall arrays, formed a quasi-rectangular microphone array. The central upper and lower
microphones were equipped with custom-built windscreens to reduce wind-induced noise
bursts.
Drone positions were tracked using an Ouster OS0 LiDAR, whose position defined the
origin of the measurement coordinate system, (x,y,z) = (0,0,0). The LiDAR was also used to
determine the microphone positions. During the measurements, the pilots and measurement
supervisor were seated behind the LiDAR. To facilitate robust tracking, a retroreflector was
attached to the X500 8 cm above the rotor plane. Retroreflector-based detection was not
feasible for the larger UBADRON because false positives from the frame and propellers

within a predefined volume. Two GoPro cameras provided visual documentation from the xand y-directions, and each measurement was initiated by an impulse generated with a start

2.3

Evaluation

Prior to the analysis of the measurement data, audio and drone position data had to be
matched. As the LiDAR-based position tracking provided discrete position estimates at an
average rate between 3 Hz and 10 Hz, the trajectories were first interpolated using a Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial (PCHIP). The resulting position data were resampled to match the sample-wise temporal resolution of the array time signals at a sampling rate of fs = 102.4 kHz. The timestamps of the microphone-array recordings and LiDAR
tracks enabled an initial approximate matching of the individual measurements. To refine
the temporal alignment, the real-time rendered position data were then visually matched
with the corresponding GoPro footage, and the offset relative to the start-flap impulse at the
beginning of each measurement was determined. This impulse was then identified in the
microphone-array time signals and used to align the position data with the acoustic recordings. A residual uncertainty in the exact temporal alignment remains.
After interpolation, resampling and time alignment the UAV trajectory was represented by
the sample-synchronous position vector xUAV [n] = (x[n], y[n], z[n]), where n denotes the audio
sample index. Offsets were applied to the z positions to match the rotor planes. An offset
of z = −8 cm was applied to the X500 position data due to the reflector cube positioned
above the rotor plane, while an estimated offset of z = +15 cm was added to the UBADRON
position data.
2.3.1

Hovering Scenarios

To enable the calculation of psychoacoustic metrics in addition to level-based directivities, a
backpropagation method was applied to retain time-domain signals. In this paper, only the
4

quasi-rectangular subarray consisting of 33 microphones located around x = 4.5 ± 0.25 m
was considered for the analysis (see Figure 3). The UAV was considered to be hovering
inside the subarray when it was localized within the range of x = 4.5 ± 0.25 m. The data
selection and propagation procedure was performed in an iterative, block-wise manner. For
each block of 0.1 s duration, corresponding to a block length of n = 10240 samples with an
overlap of 50%, the x-position of the UAV was evaluated to determine whether the source
was located within the lateral extent of the microphone array. Blocks satisfying this condition
were assigned to the array plane at x = 4.5 m and corrected for propagation effects.
For each block, the UAV was then assumed to be stationary, and its mean position in
the array plane (z,y) was used to determine the microphone-specific propagation distances.
The microphone signals pm [n] were then scaled to a common reference radius of r0 = 1 m to
account for spherical spreading, with the radial distance dm,r0 describing the distance from
each microphone (m) to this reference radius (r0 ). Although drones are spatially extended
sources, they were approximated as acoustic point sources for the propagation correction,
while atmospheric attenuation was neglected for the considered distances. The resulting
signals therefore represent propagation-corrected pressure signals at the reference radius,
as illustrated in Figure 3.

Figure 3: On the left: Top-down view of the array, illustrating the selected subarray plane and positional
tolerances for both microphones and drone. On the right: Exemplary backpropagation scheme for one window,
incorporating distance-based scaling to account for spherical spreading to the reference radius r0 (orange)
around the drone (cross).

Despite the use of custom-built windscreens, microphones located directly below the
UAV were frequently affected by wind-induced low-frequency noise, so that many blocks
could not be considered valid acoustic measurements. To exclude these corrupted blocks,
a simple empirical rejection criterion was defined based on the RMS scaled FFT spectra of
the backpropagated signals from microphones below the drones. In the X500 case, blocks
were rejected if any FFT bin magnitude level exceeded 70 dB below 100 Hz. For UBADRON,
the threshold was increased to 77 dB and the frequency range lowered to below 40 Hz due
to its higher overall sound pressure levels and more low-frequency content. The criterion is
illustrated in Figure 4 for the hovering cases.
In addition to the residual motion of the UAV during nominally quasi-static hovering, which
resulted in continuously changing relative angles between the source and the microphones,
the chosen array geometry did not allow for equiangular sampling of the source directivity for
any fixed source position, unlike measurement geometries used in previous work (C. RamosRomero, Green, Torija, & Asensio, 2023; Herold, 2022). Since irregular angular sampling
is inconvenient for level averaging and directivity modelling, both of which benefit from data
represented on an equiangular grid, a blockwise angular binning procedure, comparable to
that used in a directivity-sphere measurement with more regular angular sampling (Herold,
2022), was applied. The radiation direction in the considered vertical subarray plane was

discretized using an equiangular grid over the angle θ with freely selectable angular spacing
∆θ as pictured in Figure 5. For each analysis block, the relative angular position of the UAV
with respect to each microphone was calculated and assigned to the corresponding angular
bin. Fractional-octave-band sound-pressure levels according to IEC 61260-1 (IEC, 2014)
and power spectral densities were then collected from the corresponding microphone signals
and energetically averaged for each angular grid cell. In this way, an estimate was obtained
for every angular bin for which at least one valid microphone observation was available
(Herold & Sarradj, 2023). While smaller angular spacing improves angular resolution, larger
spacing can improve grid coverage and reduce gaps. Although this procedure regularizes
the spatial sampling, it results in an unequal number of observations per angular bin.

Figure 5: Equiangular grid used for data collection (binning procedure) with varying angular spacing ∆θ starting at 0◦ .

As receiver-based quantities, psychoacoustic metrics provide information beyond mere
sound pressure levels by quantifying perceptually relevant characteristics such as temporal
fluctuations. Since particularly for highly directional sources such as drones, calculating a
parameter at a single position may not yield a representative estimate, a psychoacoustic
characterization of the radial emission pattern was conducted to further describe the acoustic properties of the investigated drones. As the challenge of a continuously moving source
remains, and some metrics have been observed to be highly sensitive to individual analysis
windows and may vary considerably over short time intervals, the same binning procedure
as applied to the level-based metrics was employed. For each bin, the corresponding metric
values were averaged arithmetically. The analysis windows were enlarged to 1 s, keeping
the 50 % overlap, which was selected as a compromise between angular-temporal stability,
while still allowing the time-varying auditory filters of the respective models to develop sufficiently. Since different psychoacoustic models may respond differently to the same signal,
different models were considered for most sensations. Stationary loudness was calculated
6

according to ISO 532-1 (DIN and ISO, 2017), from which sharpness was derived using
the Aures and DIN 45692 weightings according to DIN 45692 (DIN, 2009). Tonality was
calculated using both the Sottek Hearing Model according to ECMA-418-2 (ECMA, 2025)
and the Aures (Aures, 1985) model. Roughness was evaluated using the Sottek Hearing
Model (ECMA, 2025) as well as the model by Daniel and Weber (Daniel & Weber, 1997).
To improve comparability between roughness models, the R90 value was derived for both
the Daniel and Weber and the Sottek Hearing Model roughness results. For metric calculation Artemis Suite by HEAD Acoustics (HEAD acoustics GmbH, 2024), as well as open
source implementations from the Python packages MoSQITo (Green Forge Coop, 2024),
and sottek-hearing-model (Lotinga, Torjussen, & Felix Greco, 2025) as well as MATLAB Toolbox SQAT (Greco, Merino-Martínez, & Osses, 2025) were used.
2.4

Dynamic manoeuvres

Since in the case of dynamic manoeuvres such as forward flight or vertical ascent–descent
the drone can clearly not be considered stationary at any time for backpropagation, the evaluation procedure differed. A longer measurement segment containing multiple fly-through
and ascent–descent manoeuvres was selected, and a time-domain retarded-time de-Dopplerization procedure (Howell, Bradley, McCormick, & Brown, 1986) was applied to all microphone signals. Resampling was performed using third-order B-spline interpolation (Graham,
2018) between samples, after applying a low-pass filter at 40 kHz to avoid aliasing artefacts.
The spherical-spreading correction to the reference radius (r0 ) was then applied sample-wise
to the de-dopplerized time signals.
Typically in such analyses, the determination of noise hemispheres or spheres is the
general objective (Herold, 2022; C. Ramos-Romero et al., 2023), representing the threedimensional counterpart of the binning procedure used here. In the present study, however,
this was not pursued, and directivity was evaluated in two dimensions within the measurement plane. For forward flight, ascent, and descent, three analysis windows were selected
for each manoeuvre. These segments were analysed separately, and no averaging over multiple flights was performed. The same wind-rejection criteria as for hovering were applied in
the evaluation of dynamic manoeuvres.
In the fly-through case, 0.5 s subsegments were selected during which the drone passed
through the quasi-rectangular microphone array in one direction, typically covering a range
from approximately 1 m before to 1 m after the subarray. The two-dimensional directivity
was then obtained directly from the already backpropagated signals, effectively representing
a snapshot of the radiation characteristics during the fly-through. Binning was applied to the
single window, since the vertical position of the drone along the fly-through trajectory was
considered sufficiently stationary over the selected subsegments.
For the vertical manoeuvres, again 0.5 s subsegments were selected from individual
ascent–descent flights during which the drone exhibited approximately steady ascending or
descending motion. Since the vertical position was constantly changing, the same binning
procedure as for the hovering cases, with a window length of 0.1 s and an overlap of 50%,
was applied.
Owing to the limited flight distances available in the anechoic chamber, the selected
manoeuvres included acceleration and deceleration phases and were therefore not fully stationary in speed. Averaging over multiple flights with different instantaneous velocities was
avoided to preserve the interpretability of speed-dependent directivity features. By contrast,
the hovering cases provided quasi-stationary operating conditions, for which averaging was
considered appropriate.

In general, the larger UBADRON exhibits substantially higher octave-band sound pressure levels than the X500. Its acoustic energy is concentrated primarily in the lower-frequency
bands and decays towards higher frequencies, whereas the X500 shows a spectral shift towards higher-frequency content. The X500 exhibits a relatively broad directivity pattern in
the octave band containing the blade-passing frequencies, at approximately 220 Hz, with
reduced vertical directivity and a more pronounced horizontal radiation. This pronounced
horizontal directivity in the BPF band is consistent with previous observations for quadcopters (Herold, 2022; Herold & Sarradj, 2023; Herold & Benz, 2026). Below the first BPF,
the radiation is largely omnidirectional. At 500 Hz, 1 kHz, and 2 kHz, the directivity becomes
distinctly dipole-like. At higher frequencies, this dipole structure becomes less pronounced
and transitions towards a more oval pattern.

In comparison, for UBADRON, the 63 Hz band containing the BPFs is less dominant
and shows a broader, more rounded directivity pattern rather than pronounced horizontal
directivity. The characteristic dipole-like directivity is instead observed across most midfrequency bands. It should be noted that the BPF band of the UBADRON partly falls below

outliers or wind-induced disturbances.

The bin-averaged psychoacoustic metrics are shown in Figure 8; variability is quantified by the standard deviation. Both drones exhibit dipole-like loudness patterns, with the
larger UBADRON being almost twice as loud as the X500 in most directions. Even though
there is a large loudness difference, this is not reflected in the Aures’ sharpness values.
The DIN 45692 sharpness weighting, which is independent of loudness, indicates higher
sharpness for the smaller UAV.
According to Aures’ tonality model, both drones exhibit stronger tonal radiation in the horizontal direction. In contrast, Sottek’s loudness-based tonality metric reveals oblique tonal
asymmetries for the smaller drone, which also exhibits substantially higher overall tonality
values. Regarding roughness, the smaller X500 shows higher values in the vertical directions, whereas the larger UBADRON exhibits increased roughness in the obliquely downward directions. Overall, the roughness values of the larger UBADRON are substantially
higher than those of the smaller X500, reaching up to 2 asper. This is not unexpected,
as the UBADRON’s BPFs are located around 63 Hz, a frequency range in which roughness may become particularly relevant due to the corresponding modulation rates (Fastl &
Zwicker, 2007).

4.

C ONCLUSION

Two UAVs differing substantially in size, configuration, and acoustic signature were measured under controlled yet realistic operating conditions in an anechoic chamber and analysed with respect to their acoustic and psychoacoustic directivity. The smaller X500 quadcopter exhibited pronounced horizontal radiation in the BPF octave band. This directivity
characteristic was observed not only in hover, but also during ascent and descent. The evaluated manoeuvres indicated a tendency towards increased sound pressure levels during
ascent, particularly at higher ascent speeds, and reduced levels during descent compared
with hover. The substantially larger UBADRON hexacopter produced considerably higher
octave-band sound pressure levels, with its acoustic energy concentrated primarily at lowerand mid-frequency ranges, showing a less pronounced horizontal sound radiation in its first
BPF band.
The considered psychoacoustic metrics captured the differences between the two acoustic signatures well. Relative to its loudness, the smaller X500 produced a sharper, substantially more tonal, and less rough sound than the larger UBADRON. Stationary psychoacoustic metrics, such as loudness and sharpness, the latter being derived from stationary
loudness, showed only limited variability. Greater variability was observed for roughness and
tonality, particularly when evaluated using the Sottek Hearing Model.
Although individual analysis windows often already yielded reasonably stable directivity
patterns for both the octave-band levels and the psychoacoustic metrics, the applied binning
procedure proved effective in stabilising the resulting directivity estimates. Overall, the results show that distributed, non-circular microphone arrays enable directivity measurements
under realistic flight conditions in an anechoic chamber, where only limited space is available. However, fully representative outdoor flight speeds could not be investigated. Future
measurement campaigns could improve the microphone spacing to achieve a more nearly
equiangular coverage. In addition, wind-overload effects could be reduced by increasing the
microphone distances below the drones.
5.

O UTLOOK

The measured directivities will be transferred into a more complete and application-oriented
representation. This includes the derivation of noise (hemi-)spheres from the available data,
for which the remaining microphones of the array can be included and suitable interpolation strategies are required for missing angular positions. For this purpose, the directivities
can be resolved in one-third-octave bands. This also allows assessing whether vertical rotational symmetry about the z-axis can be assumed for auralisation (Wunderli et al., 2022).
In addition, the spectral and psychoacoustic characteristics can be investigated in greater
detail. This includes a closer analysis of different flight manoeuvres, as well as an extension
of the psychoacoustic evaluation to additional metrics and psychoacoustic annoyance models. Finally, the resulting directivity data for different flight manoeuvres could be combined
with existing drone-synthesis approaches (Dreier & Vorländer, 2024; C. A. Ramos-Romero,
Green, & Torija Martinez, 2024), enabling more realistic auralisations of UAV flyovers with
manoeuvre-dependent sound radiation.
