This study investigated the effects of testing environmental conditions on noise characteristics of
drones as an unmanned aerial vehicle to develop standardized test procedure. Since standardized
test procedure for evaluating drone noises has not been fully established, basic noise levels of
drones are measured in anechoic chamber based on EU Directive and ISO 3744. Several commercial drones with various weights were chosen for the noise tests. Sound power levels and directional
noise characteristics of the hovering drones were derived. As a result, sound power levels, LwA, of
the drones were ranged from 72.4 to 97.3 dBA. Noise reduction of almost 4.2 dB by low-noise propeller was observed when a propeller guide was not applied for safe purpose. However, safeguard
did not show significant noise reduction. Then, in-situ outdoor test of drone noise was carried out to
compare the effects of test environmental conditions. Drones for the field tests were operated in the
similar manner with laboratory condition, hovering at 0.5 m to 5 m high from ground floor. As results, 5-m off sound pressure levels, LpA, of two drones were ranged by hovering heights from 76.0
to 77.0 dBA, and 59.2 to 62.7 dBA, respectively. In addition, differences of drone noise characteristics by the test environmental conditions were discussed.

Keywords: drone noise, UAV, sound power level, anechoic room, outdoor noise

1.

Introduction

The unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) industry, which has begun to be developed for military purposes, is currently the most promising technical field and is widely used to meet private demand for indus1

trial and leisure purposes [1-2]. Unmanned aircraft used in the private sector are collectively referred to
as drones. It can be easily found in famous electronic shopping malls and local markets. It is reported
that buzzing sounds from drones make people more annoy than the ground vehicles [3]. Although several researchers tried to figure out noise characteristics of drones in various approaches [4-6], there is
no relevant standardized procedure to quantify drone noise. Therefore, it is urgent to establish performance standards and management measures to safely manufacture and operate it because it is a small
aircraft subject to aviation law and has hazards such as safety accidents, noise from operation, and privacy [1]. Some research showed drone noise properties from outdoor or laboratory conditions [7-8].
However, there is no comparison on test environmental conditions with the same testing drone object.
In this study, the effects of testing environmental conditions on noise characteristics of drones are
investigated. Regarding a commercial drone, two test environments of an anechoic room as a laboratory condition and an outdoor ground with artificial grass as an in-situ field condition are compared.
Then, the effects of drone weights on noise properties in a laboratory condition and the effects of drone
hovering heights were discussed.

2.

Materials and method

2.1 Environmental conditions
Figure 1 shows the pictures of the test environments. As a laboratory condition, the box-type semianechoic room, which was in Daejeon, South Korea was employed as shown in Figure 1(a). The inner
dimension was 8.2 m in length, 7.0 m in width and 4 m in height from the metallic ground surfaces.
Cut-off frequency of the room was 63 Hz. As a in-situ field condition, the football field covered by
artificial grass in Guri, South Korea was employed as shown in Figure 1(b). Size of the outdoor fields
was 101 m in length and 62 m in width. Background noise level in the anechoic room was less than 15
dB(A). However, the football fields showed slightly high background noise level of about 50 dB(A)
due to nearby road traffic sources.

2.2 Measurement method and equipment
Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/945 specifies noise test code for the hovering drones
at 0.5 m high on reflecting ground in a semi-anechoic room [9]. This procedure follows the requirements on ISO 3744 [10] with ten microphones as shown in Figure 2(a). However, there were some possibilities of physical interference between some unstably hovering drones and the tenth microphone on
the top of the measuring hemisphere in a laboratory condition. Accordingly, this study followed the
noise test code for measuring airborne noise emitted by equipment for use outdoors with six microphones based on the Directive 2000/14/EC as shown in Figure 2(b) [11]. In addition, simplified measThe 28th International Congress on Sound and Vibration (ICSV28), 24-28 July 2022

3.

Results

3.1 Anechoic room tests
Table 1 shows the measurement results of the drones in the anechoic laboratory condition with the
summarized test configurations. Sound power levels of the drones measured from the six microphones
in accordance with the Directive 2000/14/EC [11] ranged from 72.4 to 97.3 dBA. However, two microphones of M5 and M6 at 2.4 m height among the six microphones shows higher sound levels by
about 3 dB on average than other four microphones at 1.5 m height. This can be seen as the effect of
the propeller wind acting in a downward direction. Accordingly, sound power levels of the drones
measured from the four microphones at 1.5 m height are calculated as 71.2 to 95.6 dB, which is lower
by 0.9 to 1.9 dB than those from the six microphones.
As for the results of Phantom 4 Pro V2.0, low noise type propeller showed reduced sound power
levels of 4.2 and 2.8 dB, respectively, without and with safeguard. On the other hands, safeguard
showed not consistent results as 0.9 dB reduction for the low noise propeller but 0.5 dB amplification
for normal propeller. In addition, Mavic Air showed 2.7 dB amplification by use of safeguard.
Simultaneously, simplified measurements with four sound level meters as shown in Figure 2(c) were
carried out to compare the results with field condition. As shown in Table 1, sound power levels at 0.5
m height in four orthogonal directions ranged from 65.2 to 89.9 dB, which is smaller by 4.6 to 7.4 dB
than those by the Directive 2000/14/EC method. When considering the results by the four points from
M1 to M4 as mentioned above, this difference is reduced to 3.5 to 6.0 dB. This difference seems to be
because of the measurement height rather than the effect of the number of measurement points.

3.2 Outdoor tests
Figure 5 shows the results of sound pressure levels of two drones measured at the outdoor condition.
During the measurements, two drones hovered at various heights of 0.5 to 5 m from the ground for at
least 15 s. Then, it was marked as coloured parts in Figure 5. Noise levels of the custom-made octocopter drone model ranged from 76.0 to 77.0 dBA by hovering height, which is greater by 20 dB or more
than the background noise level. However, noise levels of the quadcopter drone model ranged from
59.2 to 62.7 dB by hovering height, which shows only 10 dB difference to the background noise level.

Discussions

4.1 Effects of drone weights on noise levels in anechoic condition
Figure 6 shows relationship between the A-weighted sound power levels measured in the semianechoic room and the drone weight with and without battery in terms of the averaged microphone
numbers. The noise level of the drones increased in proportion to the logarithmic value of its weight
regardless of the presence or absence of a battery. The results of linear regression analysis between the
logarithmic value of the drone weight and the noise showed that the weight when there was no battery
showed a somewhat high explanatory power, and the effect of the average value according to the number of measurement points was not noticeable except for the difference in absolute values. However,
noise levels of the Mavic 2 Enterprise and the Mavic 2 Pro models were significantly lower than those
of similar linear regression analysis trends despite a weight equivalent to around 0.9 kg when included
in the battery. Therefore, since low noise can be secured even if drone noise shows characteristics proportional to weight, continuous efforts are needed to develop low-noise drones through component design such as motors and propellers.

4.2 Effects of drone hovering height on noise levels in outdoor condition
Figure 7 shows the relationship between the A-weighted sound pressure levels measured in the outdoor condition and the hovering height from ground. Due to field conditions, sound level meters could
not be installed at 0.5 m height as the same manner with semi-anechoic room. Thus, the measurement
was carried out at 1.5 m with microphone wind shield by applying a common environmental noise
measurement method. When measured at a height of 1.5 m, the noise of the drone tended to increase as
the height increased. Noise level of the custom-made octocopter drone increased by about 1 dB when
the hovering height of the drone increased from 0.5 m to 5 m. Meanwhile, noise level of the quadcopter
drone increased by about 3.5 dB when the hovering height of the drone increased from 0.5 m to 5 m. A
drone user reported that noise levels of the vertically flying drone decreased as the altitude above 7.62
m (25 ft) [13]. In this study, noise increased as the altitude increased due to the influence of aerodynamic noise by propellers since only maximum 5 m of flight altitude was considered due to the limitation of the field measurement conditions. In future studies, experiments on the critical distance affected
by aerodynamic noise are needed.
4.3 Effects of test environmental conditions on noise levels for the same drone
Figure 8 shows the calculated results of the A-weighted sound power levels for the same quadcopter
drone model (Phantom 4 Pro) according to the test environmental conditions. Even the drone model
and the hovering heights were the same, a direct comparison is still not easy because the measuring
heights are different from 0.5 m and 1.5 m. However, A-weighted sound power level of the drone hovering at 0.5 m in the anechoic condition and that of the drone hovering at 1 m in the outdoor condition
were 82.6 dB and 82.5 dB, respectively. Therefore, it may be possible to measure drone noise as much
as a laboratory even outdoors if only the difference from the background noise can be sufficiently secured.

5.

Conclusion

In this study, the characteristics of drone noise were evaluated according to the measurement environmental conditions. The noise characteristics of various types of the drone models in the semianechoic room were measured during hovering actions at 0.5 m height according to the EU methods.
As results, sound power levels of the tested drones ranged from 72.4 to 97.3 dBA. Additionally, noise
reduction effects of low-noise propeller and safeguard were discussed based on the laboratory measurement results. In the outdoor condition, two drone models were tested, then effects of hovering height
and testing environment conditions were discussed. Drone noise is basically proportional to the weight
including the battery, but the possibility of reducing the noise level was confirmed through detailed
designs such as low noise propellers.

Accordingly, based on the results of this study, it is possible to contribute to the development of test
standards that can quantitatively evaluate drone noise through continuous research in the future. In addition, it is necessary to develop a noise measurement method and an outdoor demonstration noise
measurement method during actual operation in a laboratory such as an unscented room to evaluate the
operating performance of devices excluding external environmental effects. Furthermore, it is necessary to develop a simple measurement and prediction technology for major noise generating parts, and
to study a technology that can predict the overall noise level by combining low noise factors in product
development.
